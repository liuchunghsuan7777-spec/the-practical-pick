How to Lower Your Electric Bill in Summer: 7 AC Tips That Work

Quick answer: At the U.S. average electricity rate, running central air conditioning costs roughly $8–$9 a day (about $240–$270 a month), and many households spend $400–$800 cooling their home over the summer. The fastest, proven ways to bring that down: set the thermostat to 78°F when you're home, run a ceiling fan so you can nudge it up a few degrees, and keep the filter clean.

If your summer power bill makes you wince every time it lands, you're not imagining it. Electricity rates have been climbing, and the AC is usually the single biggest reason your bill jumps from June through August. This guide breaks down what running the AC actually costs, how to estimate it for your home, and seven changes that genuinely move the needle.

One note up front: rates and equipment vary a lot by utility and state, so treat the dollar figures here as ballpark numbers. The formula and the savings tips, though, work just about everywhere.

How much does it cost to run an air conditioner?

Here's the part most people skip: your AC doesn't have its own bill. It's just the biggest slice of your whole-home electricity use, which your utility charges by the kilowatt-hour (kWh) — basically, one kilowatt of power used for one hour.

The simple cost formula

You can estimate your own cooling cost with one line of math:

Cost = unit's power (kW) × hours run × your rate ($/kWh)

To use it, you need two numbers:

  • Your AC's power draw in kilowatts. A window unit might pull around 0.5–1.5 kW; a central system runs roughly 3–5 kW while the compressor is on.
  • Your rate per kWh. Look on your utility bill — it's usually listed as cents per kWh.

The U.S. average residential electricity price was about 17.3¢/kWh in 2025 and is projected to be roughly 18¢/kWh in 2026, with rates up around 5% year over year (U.S. Energy Information Administration, Short-Term Energy Outlook). What's the best thermostat setting for summer?

The single highest-leverage dial in your house is the thermostat — and the trick is counterintuitive: a smaller gap between indoor and outdoor temperature means a lower bill. The harder you make your AC fight the heat outside, the more it costs.

The U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) recommends setting the thermostat to 78°F (about 26°C) when you're home and need cooling. When you're asleep or away, let it drift higher.

Setting the temperature back 7–10°F for about 8 hours a day — say, while you're at work — can save up to 10% a year on heating and cooling (DOE Energy Saver). A programmable or smart thermostat makes this automatic so you never have to remember.

When Suggested setting Why
Home and awake 78°F (26°C) DOE's comfort/savings sweet spot
Away ~8 hrs Set back 7–10°F (e.g., 85–88°F) Up to ~10%/year savings
Sleeping Slightly higher than 78°F + a fan Comfort with less runtime


The best thermostat setting for summer is 78°F at home, with a 7–10°F setback while you're away.

One myth worth killing: cranking the thermostat way down does not cool your home faster. The AC cools at the same rate either way — you just end up overshooting and wasting energy (DOE).

7 ways to lower your summer electric bill

None of these requires a contractor or a big purchase. The percentages below come from DOE and ENERGY STAR, but actual savings vary with your home, climate, and habits — so treat them as direction, not a guarantee.


A quick checklist of seven proven ways to reduce AC energy use and lower your summer electric bill.

  1. Set the thermostat to 78°F when home. The closer to the outdoor temperature, the cheaper it runs (DOE).

  2. Run a ceiling fan. With a fan going, you can raise the thermostat about 4°F with no loss of comfort thanks to the wind-chill effect (DOE). Just remember fans cool people, not rooms — turn them off when you leave.

  3. Close blinds and drapes during the day. About 76% of the sunlight hitting your windows turns into indoor heat, so blocking it cuts the cooling load (DOE).

  4. Clean or replace the air filter. A clogged filter forces the system to work harder; swapping it out can cut energy use 5–15% (DOE). Check it monthly during peak season.

  5. Use a programmable or smart thermostat. Automating the 7–10°F setback while you're away locks in that ~10% yearly savings without any willpower.

  6. Seal leaks and skip the oven on hot days. Weatherstrip drafty doors and windows, and cook with a stovetop, microwave, or grill so you're not heating the house you're paying to cool.

  7. Maintain the unit — and consider efficiency when replacing. Keep coils and vents clear. When it's time for a new system, look for a high SEER2 rating and ENERGY STAR certification. Should you leave the AC on or turn it off when you're away?

This is the question everyone argues about, and on a flat rate the answer is clear: turn it up, don't turn it off.

For central AC, the consensus best practice is to raise the thermostat 7–10°F while you're away rather than shutting it down completely. DOE's setback math is where the ~10% yearly savings comes from. The reason you don't go fully off — especially in humid climates — is that heat and humidity build up while the system is idle, so it has to work much harder to recover when you get home (analysis summarized by PBS NewsHour).

Strategy What happens Verdict
Turn it fully off Heat + humidity build up; hard recovery later Not ideal in humid climates
Set it 7–10°F higher Less runtime, easier recovery Best for most homes
Leave it at your usual temp Full runtime all day Most expensive

The "it's cheaper to leave it running at full blast all day" idea is a myth on a standard flat rate. The winning move is the setback, not the full shutoff.

A quick word on variable-speed (inverter) systems

In newer central systems and heat pumps, variable-speed (inverter) compressors run longer at low output instead of slamming on and off, which is generally more efficient than older single-stage units. If you're shopping, that's a feature worth asking about — but we're keeping this qualitative on purpose, since we don't have a verified savings percentage to quote. Frequently asked questions

How much does it cost to run the AC all day?
Roughly $8–$9 a day for central AC at the U.S. average rate, or about $1 a day for a typical window unit run for several hours. To get your own number, multiply your unit's kilowatts by the hours you run it by your $/kWh rate (it's on your bill).

Is 78°F really comfortable — and does it actually save money?
Yes on the savings: a smaller indoor-outdoor gap means less runtime and a lower bill (DOE). For comfort, pair 78°F with a ceiling fan, which lets you feel about 4°F cooler than the thermostat reads.

Do ceiling fans actually lower my bill?
They help indirectly. Fans don't cool the air, but the breeze lets you set the thermostat higher for the same comfort — and that's what saves money. ENERGY STAR-certified fans move air about 20% more efficiently, too. Just turn them off in empty rooms.

Is it cheaper to leave the AC on all day or turn it off?
On a flat rate, neither extreme wins. Set it 7–10°F higher while you're away instead of turning it fully off — that's the DOE-backed sweet spot, and it avoids the humidity rebound that makes the system struggle when you return.

Does a window unit cost less than central AC?
Usually, yes — a single 12,000 BTU window unit runs around $1 a day and roughly $130 for the summer, because it cools one room instead of the whole house. Central AC is far more expensive per day but cools everything at once.
