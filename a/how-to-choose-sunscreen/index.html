How to Choose Sunscreen: SPF, Mineral vs Chemical & More

Quick answer: Pick a sunscreen labeled broad spectrum, SPF 30 or higher, and water resistant. Apply enough — about a two-finger line for your face and a shot glass (1 ounce) for your body — roughly 15 minutes before you head outside, and reapply every 2 hours (and right after swimming or sweating).

Standing in the sunscreen aisle wondering what SPF actually means, whether "mineral" is better than "chemical," and how much you're supposed to slather on? You're not alone. This guide walks you through how to choose sunscreen the way a dermatologist would frame it: what the labels mean, the real differences between formulas, how to match one to your skin and your day, and how to use it so it actually works.

One note up front: this is general information, not medical advice. If you have a skin condition, a history of reactions, or questions about a child, talk to your dermatologist or pediatrician.

What SPF and "broad spectrum" actually mean

The two most confusing things on a sunscreen label are SPF and the words "broad spectrum." They cover two different types of UV rays, so it helps to know which is which.

SPF measures UVB (sunburn) protection, while "broad spectrum" means UVA (aging) is covered too.

Term Rays it covers What those rays do
SPF UVB Sunburn, surface skin damage
Broad spectrum UVA + UVB UVA drives premature aging and wrinkles and contributes to skin cancer
  • SPF (Sun Protection Factor) measures protection against UVB — the rays that cause sunburn. SPF 30 blocks about 97% of UVB, and SPF 50 blocks about 98%. No sunscreen blocks 100%. (Source: Skin Cancer Foundation)
  • Broad spectrum means the product protects against both UVA and UVB. The AAD recommends choosing a sunscreen that is broad spectrum, SPF 30+, and water resistant. (Source: AAD)

A quick note on "PA" ratings. If you buy Korean, Japanese, or other Asian sunscreens, you'll see a PA+ to PA++++ rating. That's an Asian-market grade for UVA protection — more plus signs means more UVA defense. On U.S. products you won't usually see PA; the equivalent assurance is simply the "broad spectrum" label. (Source: Cleveland Clinic; industry/derm explainers)

A myth worth busting: higher SPF is not proportionally better. SPF 50 is not "1.6 times" SPF 30 — the percentage gap is small, and SPF numbers don't add up or multiply. For everyday life, SPF 30+ broad spectrum is plenty; for long, intense days outdoors or at the water, step up to SPF 50+. Applying enough matters far more than chasing a bigger number. (Source: Skin Cancer Foundation, AAD)

Mineral vs chemical sunscreen — which is right for you?

Sunscreens fall into two broad camps based on how they block UV. Neither is universally "better." The goal is matching one to your skin and habits.

Mineral sits on skin and reflects UV; chemical absorbs UV — neither is universally better, so match it to your skin.

Type How it works Feel White cast Irritation Tends to suit
Mineral (physical) Zinc oxide / titanium dioxide sit on skin and reflect UV Thicker Often visible Lower Sensitive, acne-prone, kids
Chemical Filters absorb UV and convert it to heat Lighter, blends in Minimal Can sting/irritate Daily wear, under makeup
  • Mineral (physical) sunscreens use zinc oxide and titanium dioxide, which sit on top of your skin and reflect or scatter UV. The FDA recognizes these two ingredients as safe and effective. They're often gentler for sensitive or acne-prone skin, kids, and pregnancy — the main trade-off is a white cast and a thicker feel. (Source: Cleveland Clinic, MD Anderson)
  • Chemical sunscreens use filters that absorb UV. They tend to be lighter, blend in better, and leave little to no white cast — which makes them easy for daily wear and under makeup. The downside: they can sting your eyes or irritate very sensitive skin. (Source: Cleveland Clinic)

The honest answer most dermatologists give: the best sunscreen is the one you'll actually use consistently, as long as it's broad spectrum, SPF 30+, and water resistant. Don't treat one type as "safer" than the other — think trade-offs and personal fit. If your skin reacts easily, patch-test a new product on a small area first. (Source: AAD)

How to choose for your situation and skin type

The same person needs different sunscreen for a commute than for a beach day. Here's how to match the formula to the moment.

Everyday wear (commuting, mostly indoors)

  • SPF 30+, broad spectrum is plenty. Since you'll wear it daily, a lightweight, low-white-cast formula (many chemical or modern mineral ones) feels least like a chore.
  • A light texture also makes it easier to reapply over makeup.

Long days outdoors and sport (hiking, exercise, hours in the sun)

  • More sun and more exposure time call for SPF 50+, broad spectrum, water resistant (80 minutes).
  • Choose something that holds up to sweat, and carry a portable format (stick or powder) so you can reapply every 2 hours.

Swimming and water (water resistant)

  • Look for a U.S. label that reads "Water Resistant (40 minutes)" or "(80 minutes)" — those are the only two durations the FDA allows. (Source: FDA)
  • Remember: nothing is waterproof. Even water-resistant sunscreen washes off, so towel dry and reapply after you get out.

Sensitive, oily, or dry skin

  • Dry skin often does well with a hydrating or cream formula; oily or acne-prone skin tends to prefer a light gel or matte finish. (This is general guidance; individual results vary.)
  • Sensitive skin: reach for mineral (zinc/titanium), fragrance-free options, and patch-test before going all in. (Source: Cleveland Clinic, Houston Methodist)

Babies and kids

  • Children have thinner skin that's more reactive, so lean on mineral sunscreen plus physical protection — clothing, hats, and shade.
  • For babies under 6 months, the FDA and AAP recommend avoiding sunscreen when possible — keep infants out of direct sun and use shade, lightweight long sleeves and pants, and a wide-brim hat. If a small area must be covered, a minimal amount of mineral sunscreen may be used, but check with your pediatrician first. (Source: FDA, AAP)

How much to apply and how often to reapply

You can buy a great sunscreen and still get burned if you under-apply — and most people do, which can cut real-world SPF roughly in half. Using it correctly matters as much as picking it. (Source: Cleveland Clinic)

How much to apply and how often to reapply: a two-finger line for the face, a shot glass for the body, every 2 hours.

  • How much — face and neck: about 1/2 teaspoon, or use the two-finger rule (a line of sunscreen along your index and middle fingers). (Source: derm explainers; Cleveland Clinic)
  • How much — body: about 1 ounce — roughly a shot glass — to cover all exposed skin (more if you're larger). (Source: AAD)
  • When: apply about 15 minutes before going outdoors so it has time to absorb. (Source: AAD)
  • Reapply: every 2 hours, and immediately after swimming, sweating, or toweling off. (Source: AAD, Cleveland Clinic)
  • Over makeup: rubbing wrecks your makeup, so use an SPF powder, stick, cushion, or setting spray and pat it on. These go on thin, so use enough.

The shot-glass and two-finger comparisons are easy rules of thumb, not exact science — face size and formula vary. The takeaway: apply generously, not sparingly.

5 common sunscreen myths

A lot of sunscreen "common sense" is just wrong. Here are five worth unlearning.

  1. "Cloudy days or being indoors means I don't need sunscreen." UVA passes through clouds and windows, so it still reaches your skin near a window or on an overcast day. (Source: AAD)
  2. "Higher SPF lets me apply less." No — under-applying cancels out the number on the bottle. Amount and reapplication matter more than the SPF figure. (Source: Cleveland Clinic, Skin Cancer Foundation)
  3. "SPF adds up — SPF 15 plus SPF 15 equals SPF 30." It doesn't. SPF values don't stack. (Source: Skin Cancer Foundation)
  4. "One morning application lasts all day." Sweat, friction, and time wear it down — reapply every 2 hours. (Source: AAD)
  5. "Water resistant means waterproof." There's no such thing as waterproof sunscreen. U.S. labels allow only 40 or 80 minutes of water resistance, after which you reapply. (Source: FDA)

When to see a dermatologist

Sunscreen is prevention, not treatment. See a dermatologist if you notice any of the following:

  • A new or changing mole, or a spot that changes color, shape, or size.
  • A spot or sore that bleeds, scabs, or won't heal.
  • A severe sunburn with blistering, fever, or feeling unwell.
  • An allergic reaction to a sunscreen — rash, swelling, or persistent stinging.

These can be signs of a skin problem that needs a professional eye. When in doubt, get it checked.

Frequently asked questions

Is SPF 30 or 50 better?
For everyday, mostly-indoor life, SPF 30+, broad spectrum is enough. For long hours outdoors or at the water, SPF 50+ gives a little more margin. Either way, applying enough and reapplying matters more than the exact number.

Mineral or chemical — which should I choose?
Neither is universally better; it depends on your skin and routine. Mineral suits sensitive, dry, or acne-prone skin (gentler, but can leave a white cast); chemical is lighter and easier under makeup (but can irritate very sensitive skin). If you've reacted before, patch-test first.

How do I reapply sunscreen over makeup?
Rubbing lotion in ruins your makeup, so use an SPF powder, stick, cushion, or setting spray and pat it on. Just remember these go on thin — apply enough to actually count.

When can babies wear sunscreen?
For babies under 6 months, the FDA and AAP recommend avoiding sunscreen when possible and relying on shade, clothing, and hats instead. After 6 months you can use it, ideally a gentle mineral formula patch-tested first — and check with your pediatrician about timing.

Do I need sunscreen indoors or on cloudy days?
Yes. UVA penetrates clouds and window glass, contributing to aging and skin damage, so it's worth wearing near a sunny window or on overcast days.

The bottom line

  • Read the label: choose broad spectrum, SPF 30+, water resistant. (SPF = UVB; broad spectrum = UVA too. On Asian sunscreens, PA++++ signals strong UVA protection.)
  • Use enough: a two-finger line for your face and a shot glass for your body, about 15 minutes before sun.
  • Reapply: every 2 hours, and right after swimming, sweating, or toweling off — even water-resistant formulas.
