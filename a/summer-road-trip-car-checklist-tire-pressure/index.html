Summer Road Trip Car Checklist: 5 Checks + Tire Pressure

Before a long summer drive, the single most important thing to get right is tire pressure: fill each tire to the cold PSI number on your driver's-door placard — not the maximum printed on the tire's sidewall. From there, four more quick checks (tread, coolant, brakes/wipers, and A/C) take care of the rest.

This guide walks you through a 5-minute, do-it-yourself pre-trip inspection you can do in your own driveway. It's general car-care information — specs and service intervals vary by vehicle, so follow your owner's manual and see a mechanic for anything that seems off.

1. What tire pressure should you run for a road trip?

Start with the question everyone asks. The right pressure for your car is the manufacturer's recommended cold PSI, and you'll find it in two reliable places: the placard on the driver's-side door jamb (the B-pillar where the door latches) or your owner's manual. It's listed in both PSI and kPa. (Source: NHTSA, Tires — nhtsa.gov/vehicle-safety/tires)

A common mistake is reading the big number molded into the tire sidewall and inflating to that. That figure is the tire's maximum pressure, not your target. Always go by the placard. (Source: NHTSA)

Check tires cold

Pressure readings are only accurate when tires are cold — meaning the car hasn't been driven for at least about 3 hours (driving heats the air and raises the reading). Check all four tires plus the spare at least once a month, and again right before any long trip. (Sources: NHTSA; Discount Tire — Road Trip Checklist)

On a multi-day trip, re-check pressure every so often, especially when the weather swings hot and cold between stops. (Source: Continental — 7 Expert Tire Tips for Your Best Road Trip)

Why low pressure is risky at highway speed

An under-inflated tire flexes more with every rotation, and that flexing builds up heat. At sustained high speed, heat plus low pressure can lead to tread separation or a blowout — exactly what you don't want on a packed summer highway. Setting pressure correctly before you leave is the simplest way to avoid it.

A note on "add 10% for long trips": some non-US car media suggest over-inflating slightly for highway driving. US safety sources (NHTSA, AAA) say inflate to the placard number, not above it — so the safe, widely recommended advice here is simply to hit the placard PSI when cold.

2. TPMS light: what it means and when to worry

That horseshoe-shaped symbol with an exclamation point on your dash is the TPMS warning light (Tire Pressure Monitoring System). It means one or more tires are significantly under-inflated — federal rules trigger it at about 25% below the placard pressure. Don't ignore it: add air to bring the tire back to the recommended PSI. (Sources: NHTSA — FMVSS No. 138; Bridgestone — TPMS Light On)

Why the light sometimes appears on a cold morning

Temperature changes pressure by roughly 1 PSI for every 10°F of swing. So a light that pops on during a chilly morning and then clears as the day warms up is usually just normal physics, not a broken sensor. Still, the right move is to check and inflate to spec while the tires are cold. (Source: Les Schwab — TPMS Light Coming On in Cold Weather)

  • Light comes on → check and add air to the placard pressure.
  • Light keeps returning after inflating → suspect a slow leak, puncture, or sensor fault → have it inspected. (Source: Bridgestone)
  • Reset steps vary by automaker — check your owner's manual rather than relying on one universal procedure.

3. Tire tread: how to do the penny test

You can gauge how worn your tires are without any tools beyond pocket change. The legal minimum tread depth is 2/32 of an inch, and at that point replacement is required in many states. (Source: Firestone Complete Auto Care — How to Check Tire Tread Depth With a Penny)

The penny test

  1. Take a penny and insert it into a tread groove with Lincoln's head pointing down into the tire.
  2. If the top of Lincoln's head is covered by the tread, you still have more than 2/32" — you're okay for now.
  3. If you can see all of Lincoln's head, the tread is at or below 2/32" — replace the tire. (Source: Bridgestone; Firestone)

Check several spots around each tire and across the width of the tread, since tires often wear unevenly. Replace if any worn area fails the test.


Left: find the cold-PSI placard on the driver's door jamb. Right: the penny test for tread wear.

For summer rain, consider the quarter test

Worn tread raises the risk of hydroplaning — when water gets between the tire and the road and you lose grip — which matters a lot during summer storms. (Source: Pirelli — Tire Tread Depth) Many tire pros suggest replacing earlier, at 4/32", for safer wet-weather traction. To check that, use a quarter with Washington's head down: if you can see all of his head, you're below 4/32". (Sources: Consumer Reports; Discount Tire)

4. Coolant and preventing summer overheating

Overheating is one of the most common summer breakdowns, and stop-and-go traffic on a hot day is the classic trigger. Before a long drive, take a quick look at your coolant level and top it off if it's low. The reservoir has ADD and FULL marks (sometimes labeled LOW/FULL) — keep the level between them. (Sources: AutoZone — How to Check Your Coolant Level; Firestone — When Your Car Overheats)

Safety warning: Never open the radiator or coolant cap when the engine is hot. The cooling system is pressurized, and opening it can spray boiling coolant and cause severe burns. Let the engine cool for at least 30 to 60 minutes first. (Sources: AutoZone; The Drive)

How often to replace coolant varies by vehicle — many modern long-life coolants last well beyond older rules of thumb. Follow your owner's manual for the right interval rather than a fixed number.

5. Brakes, wipers, and A/C for summer storms

Summer means more rain, so stopping power and clear visibility matter. A few consumable checks before you go: (Source: AAA — Prepare Your Car for Summer Travel; Discount Tire)

  • Brakes: If you hear squealing or grinding, feel a soft or sinking pedal, or notice the car pulling to one side, get the brakes inspected before the trip.
  • Wiper blades: Replace them roughly every 6 to 12 months — intense summer sun degrades the rubber. Swap them if you see streaking, skipping, or chattering, or if the rubber is cracked or torn. (Sources: AAA; Pep Boys)
  • A/C: If cooling feels weak or there's a musty smell, check or replace the cabin air filter and have the system looked at. (Refrigerant specifics vary by vehicle — leave those to a shop.)
  • Spare tire & washer fluid: Confirm the spare is present and properly inflated, and top off the windshield washer fluid before you leave. (Source: Discount Tire)

If any of these are hard to check yourself, or something seems abnormal, the safest move is to have a qualified mechanic take a look.

Frequently asked questions

Should I let air out of my tires in summer?
No. Inflate to the cold PSI on your door placard — that's the right target year-round. Don't deliberately under-inflate for hot weather.

Is the TPMS light an emergency?
It signals that a tire is low, so add air to the placard pressure as soon as you can. A light that appears on a cold morning and clears as it warms up is usually normal. If it keeps coming back after you've inflated, get it checked for a leak or sensor problem.

How do I do the penny test?
Put a penny head-down into a tread groove. If you can see all of Lincoln's head, the tread is at or below 2/32" and the tire should be replaced.

Can I top off coolant myself?
Yes — bring it up to the FULL mark — but only when the engine is cold, to avoid burns from pressurized hot coolant. If the level keeps dropping, have the system checked for a leak.

How often should I replace wiper blades?
Roughly every 6 to 12 months, or sooner if they streak, skip, or chatter across the glass.

Wrap-up: a 5-minute check for a safer trip

Run the checks in this order and you're set: (1) tire pressure to the door placard while cold → (2) tread with the penny test → (3) coolant between ADD and FULL, engine cold → (4) brakes and wipers → (5) A/C and spare. None of it takes long, so do it the day before you leave and start your road trip with peace of mind.
