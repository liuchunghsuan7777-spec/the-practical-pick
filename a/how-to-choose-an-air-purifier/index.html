How to Choose an Air Purifier: HEPA, CADR & Room Size

Quick answer: To choose an air purifier, match three things to your room — a True HEPA filter (99.97% at 0.3 microns, not vague "HEPA-type"), a high enough CADR (use the 2/3 rule: your room's square footage × ⅔ = the minimum CADR you want), and an activated carbon stage if you care about odors or smoke. Avoid ozone-generating "ionizers," and size up for a bedroom so the unit runs quietly.

Shopping for an air purifier gets confusing fast. The boxes are covered in marketing words — "HEPA-type," "ionizer," "covers up to 1,500 sq ft," "activated oxygen" — and most of them are doing more to sell you than to inform you. This guide is a brand-neutral buying framework: you'll learn the handful of specs that actually matter, how to size a unit to your space, and which features to skip. It's general home-appliance information, not medical advice.

By the end, you'll be able to look at any product page and know in about 30 seconds whether the unit is right for your room.

The 5 things that actually matter

Before we dig in, here's the whole decision in one list. Everything else on the box is mostly noise.

  1. True HEPA filtration — genuine 99.97% at 0.3 microns, not "HEPA-type."
  2. CADR high enough for your room — the 2/3 rule tells you the number.
  3. An activated carbon stage — only if you need odor, smoke, or VOC control.
  4. No ozone — skip ozone generators; make sure any ionizer can be switched off.
  5. Filter replacement cost — a cheap unit with pricey filters can cost more over time.

Now let's make each one usable.

What "HEPA" really means — and the "HEPA-type" trap

A HEPA (High Efficiency Particulate Air) filter is a dense pleated mechanical filter. By the U.S. Department of Energy standard, a true HEPA filter removes at least 99.97% of airborne particles that are 0.3 microns in diameter — that covers dust, pollen, mold spores, pet dander, and fine smoke particles. (Source: U.S. EPA, "What is a HEPA filter?")

Here's the part most people get backwards: 0.3 microns is the hardest particle size to catch — the so-called Most Penetrating Particle Size. So a HEPA filter actually performs equal or better on particles both larger and smaller than 0.3 microns. The "99.97% at 0.3 µm" rating is a worst-case benchmark, not a ceiling.

The trap is the look-alike language. Words like "HEPA-type," "HEPA-style," "HEPA-like," or "99% HEPA" are unregulated marketing phrases — they do not meet the true HEPA standard. These filters often capture only around 85–90% at 0.3 microns (a rule-of-thumb figure that varies by product), which can mean many times more of the smallest particles slipping through.

So insist on a unit that states "True HEPA" or lists the "99.97% at 0.3 µm" spec outright. Treat a bare, unquantified "HEPA" adjective as a yellow flag.

True HEPA vs "HEPA-type" at a glance

True / genuine HEPA "HEPA-type / -style / 99% HEPA"
Standard 99.97% at 0.3 µm (DOE) No standard (marketing term)
Smallest-particle capture ~99.97% often only ~85–90%
Trust it? Yes — spec is stated Treat as a red flag

CADR — the one number that tells you how fast it cleans

CADR stands for Clean Air Delivery Rate, an industry standard from AHAM (the Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers), measured in CFM (cubic feet per minute). It tells you how much genuinely clean air the unit delivers, because it combines two things at once: filter efficiency and airflow. A great filter with a weak fan still has a low CADR. (Source: AHAM Verifide; U.S. EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home.)

You'll usually see three CADR numbers:

  • Smoke — the smallest particles (roughly 0.09–1.0 micron). This is the most important number. It best reflects fine particles like wildfire smoke and fine dust.
  • Dust — mid-size particles.
  • Pollen — the largest particles, and the easiest to catch.

When you compare units, compare the smoke CADR. And look for the AHAM Verifide seal, which means the rating was independently tested — not just claimed by the manufacturer.

What size air purifier do I need? (the 2/3 rule)

This is the question everyone actually has, so here's the clean answer.

The 2/3 rule: for a room with a standard 8-foot ceiling, the unit's smoke CADR should be at least two-thirds of the room's floor area in square feet.

Room square footage × 0.667 = minimum CADR

A couple of worked examples:

  • A 10 × 12 ft bedroom = 120 sq ft → minimum smoke CADR ≈ 80.
  • A 300 sq ft living room → minimum smoke CADR ≈ 200.

Hitting that target gets you roughly 5 air changes per hour — meaning the purifier filters the room's entire air volume about five times every hour. That's the level air-quality pros generally recommend for allergy and asthma households. (Source: U.S. EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home.)

Room size → minimum CADR

Room size Minimum smoke CADR (sq ft × ⅔)
100 sq ft ~67
150 sq ft ~100
300 sq ft ~200
500 sq ft ~335

The "size up" warning. Many boxes advertise a big "covers up to X sq ft" number. That figure is often based on just 1–2 air changes per hour — barely-filtered air. For allergies, pets, or smoke, ignore the marketing coverage number and size up: pick a unit whose 2/3-rule room size meets or beats your actual room. And if your ceilings are taller than 8 feet, you have more air volume, so bump the CADR higher than the flat formula suggests.

Do you need a carbon filter? (odors, smoke, VOCs)

Here's a limit people don't expect: HEPA does not remove gases or smells. It captures particles. Odors, cooking and pet smells, the gaseous part of smoke, and VOCs (volatile organic compounds) from new furniture or paint pass right through a HEPA filter.

To deal with those, you need an activated carbon (charcoal) filter stage in addition to HEPA. And the amount of carbon matters — a thick carbon bed by weight adsorbs odors and VOCs far better and longer than a thin "carbon-coated" mesh. (Source: U.S. EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home.)

So ask yourself: do you have pets, cook often, deal with smoke, or have new furniture off-gassing? If yes, prioritize a substantial carbon stage. If you only care about pollen and dust, you can save money and skip the heavy carbon.

What to avoid: ozone and "ionizer" tricks

This is the section that protects your air quality, so don't skip it.

Avoid ozone generators. California's Air Resources Board (CARB) is blunt about it: ozone generators are not effective at cleaning indoor air, and inhaling ozone poses serious health risks. Watch for euphemisms — "activated oxygen," "super oxygen," "energized oxygen," and "saturated oxygen" usually mean ozone. (Source: CARB, Hazardous Ozone-Generating Air Purifiers.)

It gets trickier. Ionizers, electrostatic precipitators, "plasma," and some UV devices can release ozone as a by-product. Ozone can also react with VOCs in your home to form formaldehyde and ultrafine particles — in other words, create new pollutants while claiming to clean.

The safe default: buy a plain mechanical True-HEPA unit, or one that's CARB-certified. Air cleaners sold in California must be CARB-certified and emit less than 0.050 ppm of ozone, so that certification is a useful safety floor anywhere in the country. If a unit has an ionizer, make sure you can switch it off — and then leave it off.

Filters and running cost (the part people forget)

A good air purifier is usually a multi-stage stack, and each stage has its own job and lifespan:

Stage Catches Notes
Pre-filter Hair, large dust Vacuum or rinse; extends HEPA life
True HEPA Fine particles (99.97% at 0.3 µm) Replace, not washable; ~6–12 months
Activated carbon Odors, VOCs, smoke gases More carbon = better; replace sooner

A few money points worth knowing before you buy:

  • HEPA filters are generally not washable. If a filter is marketed as "washable HEPA," it's usually a lower-grade filter. Most true HEPA filters get replaced, commonly every 6–12 months depending on use and your air quality. Follow the manufacturer's indicator.
  • Carbon filters often need replacing sooner than the HEPA, and pre-filters can usually be vacuumed to extend everything's life.
  • Do the math on filters. A cheap purifier with expensive, proprietary replacement filters can cost more over a few years than a pricier unit with affordable filters. Check the replacement-filter price before you buy the machine.

Noise, placement, and smart features

One detail catches a lot of buyers off guard: CADR is measured at the maximum fan speed — which is also the loudest setting. A unit that's whisper-quiet on low may be too weak on low to actually hit its rated CADR. (Source: AHAM test method / reviewer consensus.)

The fix is the same "size up" trick: buy a purifier strong enough that it cleans your room well on a lower, quieter speed. For bedrooms, check the decibel (dB) rating and look for a sleep or night mode.

For placement, give the unit clear airflow — don't jam it in a corner or tuck it behind the couch. And run it continuously or on auto rather than in short bursts; steady operation keeps particle levels low instead of letting them rebuild. (Source: U.S. EPA, Guide to Air Cleaners in the Home.)

Smart sensors and auto modes are genuinely useful: they ramp the fan up when air quality drops and idle quietly when it's clean. Nice to have, not essential.

Which air purifier is right for you?

Match your main reason for buying to the specs that matter most.

  • Allergies / pollen → True HEPA, smoke CADR at or above the 2/3 rule, size up toward 5 air changes per hour.
  • Pets → True HEPA + a strong, cleanable pre-filter (for hair) + activated carbon (for odor).
  • Smoke / wildfire → prioritize a high smoke CADR, size up, and add carbon for the gas-phase smoke.
  • Odors / VOCs (cooking, new furniture) → a heavy activated-carbon stage is your priority, not just HEPA.
  • Bedroom → size up for quiet low-speed running, low dB, sleep mode, ionizer off.
  • Large or open-plan room → one high-CADR unit, or two smaller units placed apart.

Frequently asked questions

What size air purifier do I need for my room?
Use the 2/3 rule: multiply your room's square footage by 0.667 to get the minimum smoke CADR. A 150 sq ft room wants a CADR around 100; a 300 sq ft room wants around 200. Size up for tall ceilings, allergies, or smoke.

What is a good CADR rating?
"Good" is relative to your room. A CADR that meets or beats the 2/3 rule for your square footage is the target. Compare the smoke number specifically, and prefer an AHAM Verifide rating.

Is True HEPA better than HEPA-type?
Yes. True HEPA is certified to 99.97% at 0.3 microns. "HEPA-type" is an unregulated marketing term and often captures only around 85–90% at that size, letting far more fine particles through.

Do air purifiers remove odors and smoke?
A HEPA filter removes the particles in smoke but not the gases or smell — those need an activated carbon stage. For odor, smoke gases, and VOCs, choose a unit with a substantial carbon filter.

How often do I change an air purifier filter?
True HEPA filters are typically replaced every 6–12 months (they're not washable). Carbon filters often need replacing sooner, and pre-filters can usually be vacuumed. Follow the model's filter indicator.

Are ionizing or ozone air purifiers safe?
Skip ozone generators — CARB says they're ineffective and inhaling ozone is a health risk. Watch for "activated oxygen" euphemisms. Choose a CARB-certified unit, or a mechanical True-HEPA model with the ionizer switched off.

Should I run an air purifier 24/7?
Running it continuously (or on auto) keeps particle levels steadily low. That's why sizing up for quiet low-speed operation matters — it lets you leave it on without the noise.

The bottom line

Choosing an air purifier comes down to three specs and one thing to avoid:

  • True HEPA (99.97% at 0.3 µm — never settle for "HEPA-type").
  • Enough CADR for your room (square footage × ⅔ = your minimum, and size up).
  • Activated carbon if odors, smoke, or VOCs are on your list.
  • No ozone — buy CARB-certified or keep any ionizer switched off.

Get those right, factor in replacement-filter cost, and you'll buy once and breathe easier — without paying for marketing buzzwords you don't need.
