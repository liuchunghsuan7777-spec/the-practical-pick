How to Choose a Dash Cam (Resolution, Rear & Parking Mode)

Resolution, front vs front-and-rear, storage, and parking mode — pick a dash cam that captures what matters.

A dash cam quietly records your drive so you have clear video evidence if something goes wrong on the road. Picking the right one comes down to a handful of features that matter more than the marketing language on the box.

What to look for

The specs can feel overwhelming, but a few core features determine whether a dash cam will actually be useful when you need it. Focus on these before anything else.

Video resolution. Higher resolution means sharper footage and a better chance of reading a license plate or street sign after the fact. For most drivers, a camera that records clean, detailed video in good light is enough, but pay attention to how the footage holds up when you zoom in, since that is when small details become evidence.

Front-only vs. front-and-rear. A front dash cam covers the road ahead and is the simplest, most affordable place to start. If you also want to capture tailgaters, rear-end collisions, or what happens behind you while parked, a front-and-rear dash cam adds a second camera for the back window. Rideshare and delivery drivers sometimes go further and look for interior coverage as well, but for most people the front-and-rear setup strikes a good balance.

Field of view. This describes how wide an area the lens captures. A wider angle takes in more of the adjacent lanes and sidewalks, which helps with side-swipes and cut-ins. Extremely wide lenses can distort the edges of the image, so a moderate, well-balanced field of view is usually the more practical choice.

Night performance. A lot of incidents happen after dark, so how a camera handles low light is worth weighing carefully. Look for footage that stays usable at night without turning bright headlights into an unreadable glare. Features aimed at improving low-light capture can make a real difference here, though the only reliable test is seeing sample footage from actual nighttime driving.

Parking mode and hardwiring. A dash cam with parking mode keeps watching while your vehicle is off, which is helpful for hit-and-runs in lots or door dings on the street. Parking mode typically needs a constant power source, so it is often paired with a hardwiring kit that taps into your fuse box instead of relying on the 12V outlet. Many kits include protection that stops the camera from draining your battery too far, but it is still smart to understand how the mode is powered before you rely on it.

Storage and SD cards. Dash cams record in a loop, overwriting the oldest clips once the card fills up, so card capacity affects how much footage you keep before it recycles. Use a high-endurance microSD card designed for continuous recording, since ordinary cards can wear out quickly under constant writing. Check the maximum card size a given camera supports, and get in the habit of reformatting the card periodically.

GPS. Built-in or add-on GPS stamps your footage with location and speed data. That context can be valuable when you need to show exactly where and how fast you were traveling, and it makes reviewing trips much easier.

Who should buy what

If you mostly want peace of mind on your daily commute and prefer a straightforward setup, a front dash cam covers the essentials without much fuss. It is the easiest to install and the least expensive way to get reliable footage of the road ahead.

If you deal with heavy traffic, park on busy streets, or simply want the fuller picture, a front-and-rear dash cam is the stronger pick. Capturing both directions protects you in rear-end situations, which are common and often disputed.

If your vehicle spends a lot of time parked in public lots or on the street, prioritize a dash cam with parking mode and plan for hardwiring so it can keep watch while you are away. Drivers who leave a car unattended for long stretches tend to value this more than anything else.

Rideshare, delivery, and long-distance drivers often benefit from combining these features: rear coverage, parking protection, and GPS all at once. Casual drivers can usually keep it simple and add features later if their needs change.

Bottom line

Start by deciding how much coverage you truly need, then match the resolution, field of view, and night performance to how and where you drive. A front dash cam is a solid entry point, a front-and-rear dash cam adds valuable protection front to back, and a dash cam with parking mode watches over your vehicle even when you are not in it. Pair whichever you choose with a quality high-endurance card, and you will have dependable footage ready when it counts.
