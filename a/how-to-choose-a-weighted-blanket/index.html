How to Choose a Weighted Blanket (Weight & Fabric)

Weight, size, and fabric explained — pick a weighted blanket that feels right and stays comfortable.

A weighted blanket adds gentle, even pressure across your body, and many people simply find that reassuring and cozy at the end of the day. Picking the right one comes down to a few practical choices: how heavy it should be, how big, and what it's made of.

What to look for

The most talked-about spec is weight, and there's a widely used rule of thumb: aim for roughly 10% of the user's body weight. So a person around 150 pounds would look at something in the 15-pound range. It's a starting point, not a hard rule. If you're between sizes or unsure, many people lean a little lighter for comfort rather than heavier.

Just as important is sizing the blanket to the person, not the bed. A weighted blanket is meant to rest on top of the individual so the fill stays draped over the body. If you size it to cover a whole mattress the way a normal comforter does, the weight spills over the edges and pulls unevenly. For a shared bed, two separate blankets usually work better than one oversized one.

Inside, most modern options use glass beads as the fill. Glass beads are small, dense, and quiet, so they add weight without much bulk and tend to distribute smoothly. Some older or budget designs use plastic pellets, which are lighter for the same weight and can feel lumpier. Look for quilted stitching in a grid pattern too, since that's what keeps the beads from shifting and pooling in one corner.

A dependable standard weighted blanket usually pairs glass-bead fill with a soft, sewn-through cover and a weight that lands near that 10% guideline. That combination is the easy default for most adults who just want steady, even coverage.

Fabric and feel

Fabric changes the whole experience. Cotton is breathable and easy to live with year-round. Minky and fleece covers feel plush and warm, which is great in winter but can trap heat. Some blankets are reversible, with a cool-touch side and a cozy side, so you can flip them by season. If you tend to run warm, pay attention to the outer cover as much as the fill, because a heavy, fuzzy shell will hold warmth no matter what's inside.

Cooling and care

Heat is the number-one complaint people have with heavier blankets, since more material naturally holds more warmth. If you sleep hot or live somewhere humid, look specifically for a cooling weighted blanket. These typically use breathable cotton, bamboo-derived fabrics, or moisture-wicking cool-touch covers designed to move air and pull warmth away rather than trap it.

Care matters more than shoppers expect. A heavy blanket soaked with water can be a lot to handle, and not every model is machine-friendly. The most convenient designs come with a removable, washable duvet-style cover so you launder the outer layer often and wash the weighted insert only occasionally. Always check the label: many inner blankets need cold water, a gentle cycle, and low or no heat drying to protect the beads and stitching. Confirm your machine can handle the loaded weight before you toss it in, and spot-cleaning is fine for small spills.

Kids and safety

Children aren't just smaller adults when it comes to weight. A weighted blanket for kids is built lighter and sized down so it fits a child's frame, and the same 10% guideline should be applied to the child's actual body weight, then rounded down if you're unsure. Make sure a child can easily move the blanket off on their own. As a general safety point, weighted blankets are not appropriate for babies, toddlers, or very young children, and it's always sensible to check with your pediatrician before using one with a kid. Look for secure stitching and a snug cover so no beads can work loose.

How many to buy

Because these blankets are personal, households often end up with more than one. You might keep a breathable option for summer, a plush one for winter, and a right-sized version for a child. Buying per person also lets you match each blanket's weight to each body rather than compromising on a single shared size.

Bottom line

Start with weight at about 10% of body weight, size the blanket to the person instead of the mattress, and favor quiet glass-bead fill with even quilting. From there, let your climate guide the fabric: a plush cover for warmth, or a cooling weighted blanket if you sleep hot. Choose a washable design you'll actually maintain, and size kids' blankets down to fit them safely. Get those basics right and the rest is just picking a color you like.
