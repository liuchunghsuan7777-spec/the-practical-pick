How to Choose a Vacuum Sealer (Keep Food Fresh Longer)

Seal strength, bag type, and handheld vs countertop — pick a vacuum sealer that cuts food waste.

A vacuum sealer pulls the air out of a bag before sealing it shut, which slows down the spoilage, freezer burn, and staleness that air causes. If you buy in bulk, cook ahead, garden, hunt, or fish, the right machine pays for itself in food you no longer throw away.

External vs. chamber sealers

Most home cooks use an external (edge) sealer. You place the open end of a bag into the machine, it draws out the air, and it heat-seals the bag closed. These are affordable, compact, and simple to run. Chamber sealers are a different animal: you set the whole bag inside a rigid chamber, and the machine removes air from the entire space at once. Chamber models handle liquids and delicate items more gracefully and use cheaper flat bags, but they cost far more and take up serious counter space. For the vast majority of households, an external model is the practical pick, and this guide focuses there.

What to look for

Seal strength and reliability. The seal is the whole point. Look for a machine that produces a wide, even seal bar and lets you run a second seal for extra security. A double seal is cheap insurance on anything headed for the freezer.

Moist and dry modes. Foods vary in moisture, and a good sealer accounts for that. A dry setting works for crackers, coffee, or dried goods, while a moist (or "wet") setting adjusts the vacuum and seal timing so juices from marinated meats or fresh produce don't wreck the seal. Being able to switch between the two makes a real difference in day-to-day results.

Bag compatibility and ongoing bag cost. This is the part shoppers often overlook. External sealers need textured or embossed bags so air can channel out; plain smooth bags won't vacuum properly. Before you commit, check whether the machine takes standard vacuum sealer bags and rolls or only a proprietary size. Rolls let you cut a custom length and make your own bags, which usually lowers your cost per bag over time. Since bags are a recurring expense, a machine that accepts widely available, reasonably priced supplies will be cheaper to live with than one locked to a single brand.

Handheld vs. countertop. A countertop vacuum sealer is the workhorse. It has the suction and the heat-sealing element to handle big batches, tougher bags, and long-term freezer storage. A handheld vacuum sealer is smaller and often cordless, designed to draw air from zipper-style or valved bags and containers. It's fast, easy to stash in a drawer, and great for leftovers, snacks, or resealing something you opened yesterday. Many people end up using both: the countertop unit for freezer batches and the handheld for quick, everyday jobs.

Sous-vide use. If you cook sous vide, a strong, consistent seal matters even more, because bags sit in a warm water bath for hours. An external sealer paired with sturdy bags works well here; just leave enough room at the top of the bag and consider a double seal so nothing loosens during a long cook.

Everyday practicality. Small things add up: a removable drip tray that catches liquid and rinses clean, an accessory hose port for jars and canisters, a built-in cutter, and cord storage all make the machine easier to actually use week after week.

What it's good for

Vacuum sealing shines when you buy meat, poultry, or fish in bulk and want to portion and freeze it without freezer burn. It's excellent for garden harvests, batch-cooked meals, and warehouse-store staples like cheese and nuts. Beyond the freezer, it keeps pantry items like coffee, grains, and dry snacks fresh longer, and it's handy for organizing and protecting non-food items too, from important documents to camping gear you want to keep dry.

A general food-safety note: vacuum sealing removes air, but it is not a substitute for proper refrigeration or freezing. Sealed perishable foods still need to be kept cold, thawed safely, and used within sensible time frames. When in doubt about how long something keeps, follow standard food-storage guidance.

Bottom line

Start with how you'll use it. If you're freezing bulk meat, meal-prepping, or cooking sous vide, a countertop vacuum sealer with moist and dry modes, a strong double-seal option, and broad bag compatibility is the right foundation. If you mostly want to keep leftovers and snacks fresh, a handheld vacuum sealer is compact and convenient, and plenty of homes keep both. Whichever you choose, factor in the running cost of vacuum sealer bags and rolls, since a machine that takes affordable, easy-to-find supplies will keep your food fresh for less over the long haul.
