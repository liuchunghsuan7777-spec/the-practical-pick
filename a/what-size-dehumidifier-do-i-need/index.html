What Size Dehumidifier Do I Need? Pint & Room-Size Guide

If your home feels muggy in summer or your basement smells damp, you've probably searched for a dehumidifier and immediately hit a wall of numbers. Let's make it simple.

Match the pints-per-day rating to your room size first, then bump the capacity up for a basement or very damp space. After that, pick compressor (best for warm rooms) versus desiccant (best for cold rooms), and you're basically done. This guide walks you through the size chart, the five things that actually matter, and which type to pick for each kind of room.

A quick note before we start: keeping indoor humidity in check is general home and comfort information, not medical advice. We'll point to trusted sources along the way.

Quick answer: dehumidifier size chart by room

The number on the box (for example, "50 pint") is the pints of water the unit can pull from the air per day under test conditions. If that rating is too low for your space, the machine will run constantly and still never get the room dry.

Here's a general starting point for sizing, based on Consumer Reports and Sylvane:

Space Approx. size Recommended capacity (pints/day)
Small room / bedroom under 600 sq ft under 30 pints
Midsize room / living area 600–800 sq ft 30–40 pints
Large / whole-floor / very damp 800–1,200+ sq ft 40+ pints
Basement typical basement 50–70 pints

Buy slightly larger than the minimum. A bigger unit dries the room faster and you empty the tank less often, which is why Consumer Reports and Tom's Guide both suggest sizing up rather than down.

Also remember it's about how wet the space is, not just floor area. A small bathroom that floods can need more capacity than a large, only-slightly-humid bedroom. Adjust your estimate up if any of these apply (per Consumer Reports / Sylvane):

  • Very damp or humid climate: add about 10 pints
  • Several people use the room regularly: add about 5 pints
  • Multiple doors and windows: add about 5 pints
  • Ceilings 11 ft or higher: add roughly 10–15%

Important — why old charts overstate the size you need. In 2020 the U.S. Department of Energy changed how dehumidifiers are tested. A unit labeled "50 pint" today removes about as much water as an old "70 pint" model did. So older online sizing charts make it look like you need more pints than you actually do. Always go by current pint ratings, not a chart from years ago. (Source: Consumer Reports.)

How to choose a dehumidifier: 5 things that matter

1. Size and pints per day (the most important factor)

Start with the chart above, then adjust up for dampness, occupants, and openings. When you're stuck between two sizes, go with the larger one — running short on capacity is the most common regret.

Compressor vs desiccant dehumidifier: a compressor condenses moisture on a cold coil (best for warm rooms), while a desiccant uses an absorbing wheel and heater (best for cold rooms).

2. Compressor vs desiccant

These are the two main technologies, and the right one depends mostly on temperature.

  • Compressor (refrigerant): Cools a coil so moisture condenses and drips into the tank. It's the most efficient and effective choice in warm rooms (about 60–72°F / 14–22°C), which makes it the standard pick for damp summers and everyday living spaces. The trade-off is that compressor units are bulkier and heavier. (Sources: Honeywell, Meaco, TechRadar.)
  • Desiccant: Uses a moisture-absorbing wheel plus a heater instead of a compressor. It keeps working well in the cold — below about 50°F (10°C), where compressors waste time defrosting — so it suits cold garages, conservatories, and unheated rooms. Desiccant models are also quieter and lighter, but the built-in heater means they use more electricity. (Sources: Meaco, TechRadar, EcoAir.)

Bottom line: for warm, humid living spaces and summer dampness, a compressor unit is the standard, safe choice. For cold or unheated spaces, or if you're very noise-sensitive, consider a desiccant model. Treat this as guidance, not an absolute rule.

3. Energy efficiency (IEF and Energy Star)

The efficiency number to look for is the Integrated Energy Factor (IEF) — liters of water removed per kilowatt-hour. Higher is better. Energy Star's minimums are about 1.4 L/kWh for units under 30 pints and 1.6 L/kWh for high-capacity models; the best 2025 portables reach roughly 1.95+ and whole-home units about 2.35+. Energy Star–certified units use about 14% less energy than non-certified ones. (Source: Energy Star.)

As a rough running-cost example, a typical 50-pint Energy Star unit draws around 480–550 W and costs about $19–$28 a month at the U.S. average rate of roughly $0.17/kWh. Treat that as a ballpark — your actual cost depends on your electricity rate, how many hours you run it, and the specific model. (Source: Perch Energy.)

Inverter models (a sub-angle): An inverter uses a variable-speed compressor that ramps up and down instead of switching fully on and off. These can save energy, run quieter at partial load (roughly 35–45 dB), and typically pay back their higher price over a couple of seasons — most worthwhile for long daily runtimes (6+ hours), basements, or year-round use. Vendors sometimes claim "up to 40%" savings; the real-world figure varies, so think of it as "saves energy and runs quieter, worth it for heavy use" rather than a guaranteed number. For a small room you only run occasionally, the premium usually isn't worth it. (Sources: vendor efficiency roundups, hedged.)

4. Noise (especially for bedrooms)

Quiet dehumidifiers land around 36–51 dB on their low setting. For reference, about 50 dB is roughly a refrigerator, and under 40 dB is close to a whisper. If you'll run it in a bedroom overnight, look for a low-decibel rating or a dedicated sleep/quiet mode. Inverter and desiccant units tend to run more quietly. (Sources: Consumer Reports, LearnMetrics.)

5. Drainage and useful features

You have three ways to deal with the collected water (Sources: Consumer Reports, Dehumidifier Buyers Guide):

  • Manual tank — you empty it by hand. Fine for light use.
  • Continuous gravity drain — a hose runs to a floor drain. The drain has to sit lower than the outlet for gravity to work.
  • Built-in pump — pushes water up and out to anywhere, like a sink or a window. Best for set-and-forget, long-runtime use such as a basement.

A few features are worth having: an auto-humidistat (holds a target humidity), auto-defrost (prevents frost on the coil in cooler conditions), and a full-tank alert/shutoff. Air-purifying or laundry-drying extras are nice-to-have, but think of them as bonuses rather than a replacement for a dedicated air purifier.

Small room / bedroom (under 300 sq ft)

A small, light, quiet unit is ideal here. Aim for around 20 pints or less, and prioritize a low noise rating or sleep mode if it'll run while you sleep.

  • Pros: compact, quiet, easy to move, lower cost
  • Cons: small tank may need frequent emptying; not enough for large spaces

Midsize / living areas (600–800 sq ft)

This is the most common, do-it-all range. Look for 30–40 pints with a compressor. It covers a single room up to a small living area comfortably.

  • Pros: versatile, plenty of models to choose from
  • Cons: may fall short if you want one unit for the whole home

Large / whole-floor / very damp (800–1,200+ sq ft)

For a whole floor or a persistently damp space, go for 50+ pints and make sure it supports continuous drainage so you're not emptying the tank constantly.

  • Pros: strong dehumidification, good for damp whole-home use, hose-drain convenience
  • Cons: larger, heavier, and uses more electricity

Basement (often cool and very damp)

Basements are the classic dehumidifier use case. Plan on 50–70 pints, and seriously consider a built-in pump so water can drain upward to a sink or out a window. If the basement runs cold, a desiccant unit may handle it better than a compressor.

  • Pros: keeps a damp basement consistently dry; pump enables hands-off operation
  • Cons: high-capacity units are bigger and pricier

Cold or unheated spaces (garage, below ~50°F / 10°C)

In genuinely cold rooms, a desiccant unit is usually the better fit because it doesn't lose performance to defrost cycles the way a compressor does.

  • Pros: works in the cold, quieter, lighter
  • Cons: uses more electricity due to the heater

Frequently asked questions

What size dehumidifier do I need for a basement?
Most basements do well with 50–70 pints per day, and a model with a built-in pump makes draining hands-off. If your basement is cold, a desiccant unit may keep up better than a compressor. (Source: Consumer Reports.)

Compressor vs desiccant — which should I get?
For warm, humid living spaces and summer dampness, a compressor is the standard pick (best efficiency in warm rooms). Choose a desiccant for cold or unheated spaces, since it keeps working below about 50°F (10°C) and runs quieter — at the cost of higher electricity use.

Do dehumidifiers use a lot of electricity?
It varies by model and runtime. A typical 50-pint Energy Star unit costs roughly $19–$28 a month as a ballpark. To keep costs down, look at the IEF (L/kWh) rating and consider an inverter model for long daily use. (Source: Perch Energy, Energy Star.)

How many pints do I need for a bedroom?
For most bedrooms, 20 pints or less is plenty. The more important choice is noise — pick a unit with a low decibel rating or a sleep mode if it'll run overnight.

What humidity level should I set it to?
Aim to keep indoor relative humidity between 30% and 50% (and always below 60%). Above roughly 60%, mold, mildew, and dust mites thrive. This is general indoor-air guidance from the EPA, not a medical claim. (Source: EPA.)

Do I need a model with a pump?
A pump isn't required, but it's very handy for long-runtime or basement use because it can push water up and out to a sink or window — no daily tank emptying. (Source: Consumer Reports.)
