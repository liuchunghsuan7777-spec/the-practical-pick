How to Replace a Furnace Air Filter: Size, MERV & Arrow

Quick answer: To replace a furnace (HVAC) air filter, turn the system off at the thermostat, slide the old filter straight out, and read the size printed on its cardboard frame (something like "16x25x1"). Buy the same size in the right MERV rating, then slide the new one in with the airflow arrow pointing toward the furnace/blower — never toward the room. Most homes need this every 1–3 months. The whole job takes a couple of minutes and usually no tools.

Here's the motivation to actually do it on schedule: replacing a dirty, clogged filter with a clean one can lower your HVAC system's energy use by an estimated 5%–15%, according to the U.S. Department of Energy. A clogged filter chokes airflow, makes the blower work harder, and quietly raises your bill. So this is a roughly $10–$25 task that pays for itself — as long as you get the size, MERV, and arrow direction right. Those three are exactly what most people get wrong, so let's walk through each.

First, Turn the System Off and Find the Filter

Two quick setup steps before you touch anything.

1. Turn the system off. Set the thermostat to off (or at least the fan to off) so the blower isn't pulling unfiltered air and loose dust into your ducts while the filter is out. It's a small thing that keeps grit off your blower and coil.

2. Find the filter and its slot. In most homes it lives in one of two places:

  • Behind a return-air grille in a wall or ceiling (the large vent that pulls air in, not the small ones that blow air out), or
  • In a slot on the furnace or air handler itself, usually right where the big return duct meets the blower.

Many homes have a single filter; some have two or more returns, each with its own filter. Check them all.

Find Your Filter Size (Read It Off the Frame)

This is the easy part, and you don't measure anything. Pull the old filter out and look at the cardboard frame — the size is printed right on it, like 16x25x1 or 20x25x1 (width × height × thickness in inches).

  • That printed number is the nominal size. Buy a new filter with the same nominal size — don't try to re-measure, because the actual cut size is often about half an inch smaller than the number on the label. Trust the printed number.
  • Snap a photo of the frame before you head to the store (or before you order). It saves a second trip.
  • A filter that's the wrong size leaves gaps around the frame, and air takes the easy path around the filter instead of through it — which defeats the whole point.

Quick tip: The most common residential sizes are 1 inch thick (16x25x1, 20x25x1, 16x20x1). Some homes have a thick 4-inch or 5-inch media cabinet instead — those use a different, deeper filter, so match the thickness too.

Pick the Right MERV (8 vs 11 vs 13)

MERV stands for Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value — a 1-to-16 scale rating how well a filter captures particles between 0.3 and 10 microns. Higher MERV means finer filtration, but also more resistance to airflow, which matters more than most shoppers realize.

Here's the part that trips people up: a higher MERV is not automatically better for your system. A filter that's too dense for a furnace that wasn't designed for it can restrict airflow, strain the blower, and cut efficiency — the opposite of what you wanted. The EPA's guidance is to upgrade to a more efficient filter as high as your system can safely handle, and to consult an HVAC pro if you're not sure. Use the table to find your lane.

MERV What it captures Best for Trade-off
MERV 8 Dust, pollen, lint, dust mites Standard homes; strong airflow; the safe default Misses fine particles like smoke
MERV 9–12 The above + finer dust, mold spores, pet dander Pet owners, mild allergies A bit more airflow resistance
MERV 13 The above + smoke, fine allergens, some bacteria-size particles Allergies/asthma, wildfire smoke areas Highest resistance — only if your system can handle it

MERV 8 Pleated Filter — Best for: most homes (safe default). The standard residential filter. Captures everyday dust, pollen, lint, and dust mites while keeping airflow strong, so it's the safe default for systems whose limits you don't know. Pros: broadly compatible, low resistance, easy on the blower, usually the cheapest. Cons: lets fine particles like smoke and the smallest allergens pass through. Match the nominal size printed on your old filter; prices vary by size and store, so confirm at the point of sale.

MERV 11 Pleated Filter — Best for: pet owners & mild allergies. A medium-efficiency step up that adds finer dust, mold spores, and pet dander to what MERV 8 catches — a common pick for homes with pets or mild allergies. Pros: noticeably better filtration with airflow resistance most modern systems handle fine. Cons: denser media clogs sooner, so check it closer to every 60 days. Confirm your system can run a medium-efficiency filter, and match the printed nominal size; pricing varies by store.

MERV 13 High-Efficiency Filter — Best for: allergies, asthma, smoke (if system allows). High-efficiency filtration that adds smoke, fine allergens, and some bacteria-size particles — useful for allergy or asthma households and wildfire-smoke areas. Pros: the finest filtration in the common residential range. Cons: highest airflow resistance, so it can strain a system not designed for it and hurt efficiency. Use it only if your equipment can handle the resistance — ask an HVAC pro for the highest safe MERV. Prices vary by size and store.

Bottom line: if you don't know your system's limits, MERV 8 to 11 is the safe, broadly-compatible range. Reserve MERV 13 for when you specifically need it (allergies, smoke) and your system can run it without choking airflow — when in doubt, ask an HVAC technician for the highest filter your equipment can safely use.

Install It the Right Way (The Arrow Direction)

This is the single most common mistake, so it gets its own section. Every disposable filter has an airflow arrow printed on the frame. The rule:

The arrow points in the direction air flows — from the room, through the filter, into the furnace/blower. So the arrow always points toward the furnace, air handler, or blower motor. Never toward the room.

How that looks by slot orientation:

  • Vertical wall or ceiling return grille: the arrow points away from you, toward the duct and blower behind the grille.
  • Horizontal slot at the furnace: the arrow points inward/up toward the blower — into the unit, with the airflow.

Seat the filter so it sits fully and snugly in the slot, with no gaps around the edges (gaps let air bypass the filter). Then close the cover or grille and turn the system back on.

Pro tip: Once you've confirmed the correct direction, draw an arrow on the furnace cabinet next to the slot with a permanent marker. You'll never have to puzzle it out again. Installed backward, the filter is less effective and can sag or collapse into the system over time.

How Often Should You Change a Furnace Filter?

The general rule from ENERGY STAR and the EPA: check the filter monthly during heavy use, and change it at least every 3 months. But the right interval depends on your filter and your household. Use this as a starting guide.

Your situation Change roughly every
Basic 1-inch pleated filter, no pets ~90 days (check monthly)
Higher-MERV 1-inch filter (denser media) ~60 days
One pet ~60 days
Multiple pets ~30–45 days
Allergies, asthma, or smokers in the home Shorter — check more often
Thick 4-inch / 5-inch media cabinet ~6–12 months (more surface area)

When in doubt, hold the filter up to a light. If you can't see light through it, or it's visibly gray and matted, replace it — don't wait for the calendar.

Furnace Filter vs. AC Filter — Are They the Same?

For most central forced-air homes, yes. Your furnace and your central air conditioner share the same air handler and the same return-air filter. So the "furnace filter" and the "AC filter" are usually the same physical filter. That's why it needs changing year-round — both winter heating and summer cooling pull air through it — not just when you fire up the heat.

What Happens If You Don't Change It

Skipping it isn't harmless. A clogged filter:

  • Restricts airflow, forcing the blower motor to work harder → higher energy bills.
  • Delivers weaker heating and cooling and lets more dust circulate through the home.
  • Adds wear that can shorten the equipment's life.
  • In cooling season, can contribute to a frozen evaporator coil that shuts the system down.

Reframed that way, a clean filter is cheap insurance for a far more expensive machine.

When to Call an HVAC Pro

DIY covers almost every filter swap. But call a professional if:

  • You can't find the filter or its slot, or your system uses a sealed whole-house media cabinet you're unsure how to open.
  • You want to upgrade MERV but aren't sure your system can handle the added airflow resistance.
  • Airflow stays weak even after a fresh filter, you hear unusual blower noise, you see ice on the AC line, or the filter turns black fast — these point to a deeper problem, not just a dirty filter.

FAQ

Which way does the arrow on a furnace filter point?
Toward the furnace, air handler, or blower — in the direction air flows (from the room, through the filter, into the unit). It should never point toward the room.

What size furnace filter do I need?
Read the nominal size printed on the old filter's frame (like 16x25x1) and buy the same. Don't re-measure — the actual size is usually about half an inch smaller than the printed number.

How often should I change my furnace filter with pets?
About every 60 days for one pet, and roughly every 30–45 days with multiple pets or if anyone has allergies. Basic filters in pet-free homes last around 90 days.

Is a higher MERV always better?
No. Higher MERV filters more finely but adds airflow resistance. Too high a MERV for your system can strain the blower and hurt efficiency. MERV 8–11 is a safe range for most homes; check with an HVAC pro before jumping to MERV 13.

Do I need to turn off the furnace to change the filter?
Yes — turn the system off at the thermostat first so the blower doesn't pull dust and unfiltered air into the ducts while the filter is out. Never run the system with no filter installed.

Are furnace filters and AC filters the same thing?
In most central forced-air homes, yes — the furnace and AC share one air handler and one return-air filter, so it's the same filter you change year-round.

Final Takeaway

Replacing a furnace filter comes down to three things people overthink: size, MERV, and arrow direction. Turn the system off, slide the old filter out, copy the nominal size off its frame, choose a MERV your system can handle (MERV 8–11 for most homes, 13 when you truly need it), and slide the new one in with the airflow arrow pointing toward the blower. Do it every 1–3 months — more often with pets or allergies — and you protect your air, your bills, and the life of an expensive machine. Call a pro only when you can't find the filter, want a MERV upgrade you're unsure about, or airflow stays weak after a fresh filter.
