How to Choose a Video Doorbell (Wired vs Wireless)

Wired vs battery, resolution, and subscription costs — pick a video doorbell that fits your door and budget.

A video doorbell lets you see and talk to whoever is at your front door, whether you're on the couch or across town. Picking the right one comes down to how it gets power, how it stores footage, and which everyday features actually matter for your home.

What to look for

The most important decision is power. A wired video doorbell taps into the low-voltage wiring your old buzzer already used, so it stays charged and always on. A wireless/battery video doorbell runs on a rechargeable pack, which makes it far easier to install but means you'll recharge it every few weeks to a few months depending on traffic and weather. If you rent, or your entry has no existing doorbell wiring, a battery model is usually the simpler path.

Video quality is the next thing to weigh. Look at both resolution and field of view. Higher resolution gives you sharper detail when you're trying to read a face or a license plate, while a wider field of view lets you see more of your porch, including packages left near the ground. Some units use a tall, portrait-shaped view so you can see a visitor head to toe, which is handy for spotting deliveries.

Storage is where the ongoing cost lives. There are two broad approaches: local storage, where clips are saved on a card or a home hub you own, and cloud storage, where footage lives on the manufacturer's servers. Cloud plans typically require a monthly or yearly subscription fee to keep and review your recorded history, and without one you may only get live view and basic alerts. Local storage avoids recurring fees but ties your footage to a device in your home. Before you buy any video doorbell, check exactly which features stop working if you skip the subscription.

A few more features round out the list. Night vision matters because a lot of doorbell activity happens after dark; most models use infrared for a black-and-white nighttime image, while some add color night view when there's a little ambient light. Package detection is a nice convenience that sends a specific alert when a box appears in view, rather than a generic motion notification. Finally, confirm existing chime compatibility. A wired unit may ring your in-home chime through the old transformer, but not every chime plays nicely, so many people add a plug-in indoor chime as a backup.

Wired vs wireless: which fits your home

The choice usually mirrors your living situation. A wired video doorbell is the better fit if you own your home, already have doorbell wiring, and want a camera that never goes dark because it's charging. Continuous power also makes features like always-on recording and instant wake-up more practical.

A wireless/battery video doorbell shines when you can't or don't want to run wires. It's popular with renters, apartments, and back or side doors with no existing circuit. The trade-off is maintenance: you'll bring the battery inside to recharge, or swap in a spare, and in very cold climates batteries drain faster. If you like the flexibility but hate recharging, note that some battery models can also be hardwired to keep the pack topped off, giving you a middle ground.

Think about installation honestly, too. Wiring in a wired unit is manageable for a confident DIYer but may call for an electrician if your transformer is underpowered. A battery model is typically a bracket, a couple of screws, and a phone app.

Getting the setup right

Match the doorbell to your Wi-Fi and your daily routine. A camera far from the router may struggle, so a strong signal at the door matters more than any single spec. Decide up front how much recorded history you actually want, since that drives whether a subscription makes sense for you. And place the device at a sensible height so it captures faces and the ground near your door without staring at the sky or the welcome mat.

It also helps to think about who else needs access. Most systems let you share a live view with family members, which is worth checking if more than one person should answer the door from their phone.

Bottom line

Start with power: choose a wired video doorbell if you have the wiring and want a set-and-forget camera, or a wireless/battery video doorbell if easy installation and flexibility matter more. From there, compare resolution and field of view, decide whether you're comfortable paying an ongoing subscription for cloud storage or prefer local storage, and make sure night vision, package detection, and chime compatibility line up with your home. Get those basics right and almost any well-reviewed video doorbell will serve you well for years.
