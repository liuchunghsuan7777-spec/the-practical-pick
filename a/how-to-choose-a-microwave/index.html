How to Choose a Microwave (Countertop vs Over-the-Range)

Size, wattage, and placement explained — pick a countertop, compact, or over-the-range microwave that fits.

A microwave is one of those appliances you use every day without thinking about it, until you're shopping for a new one and realize there are more choices than you expected. This guide walks through the practical differences so you can pick the right type and size for your kitchen and cooking habits.

Start with how you actually cook

Before comparing models, think about what you reheat and defrost most often. If you mainly warm up leftovers, coffee, and frozen meals, a basic unit will serve you well. If you steam vegetables, soften butter, or reheat delicate dishes regularly, features like sensor cooking and multiple power levels start to matter more. Your cooking style should drive the decision more than any single spec sheet.

Also consider who uses the kitchen. Households with kids often prioritize simple, clearly labeled controls, while a single cook might want quick-touch presets for a handful of go-to tasks.

Countertop vs over-the-range vs compact

The three common formats each solve a different problem.

A countertop microwave is the most flexible choice. It sits on your counter or a shelf, plugs into a standard outlet, and can move with you if you relocate. The trade-off is that it occupies usable counter space, which is precious in smaller kitchens.

An over-the-range microwave mounts above your cooktop and typically combines the microwave with a vent hood and cooktop light. This frees up counter space and gives the kitchen a built-in look, but installation is more involved, often calling for a professional, and swapping it out later is harder. It's best when you're already renovating or replacing an existing over-the-range unit.

A compact microwave is a smaller-footprint countertop model designed for dorms, offices, apartments, and secondary spaces like a basement or garage. It handles everyday reheating in a tighter package, though the smaller cavity limits the size of dishes you can fit.

What to look for

A few core specs and features separate models:

  • Capacity (cubic feet): Interior volume is measured in cubic feet. Smaller cavities suit singles and light reheating, while larger ones fit big dinner plates and casserole dishes. Measure your usual containers and check they'll rotate freely inside.
  • Wattage: Higher wattage generally means faster, more even heating, while lower wattage may take longer and heat less evenly. If speed matters to you, lean toward a higher-wattage model.
  • Size and placement: Measure the opening or counter area, including height, width, and depth, and leave clearance around vents. For over-the-range units, confirm the space above your cooktop and the venting setup before buying.
  • Presets and sensor cooking: Presets offer one-touch settings for common foods, and sensor cooking adjusts time and power based on the humidity it detects, which helps prevent overcooking. These are conveniences, not necessities, so weigh them against your budget.
  • Turntable: Most models use a rotating glass turntable for even heating. Some offer a flatbed design without a turntable, which can fit rectangular dishes more easily and is simpler to clean.
  • Controls: Choose between button, dial, or touchpad interfaces. Look for a layout you find intuitive, a readable display, and useful extras like a kitchen timer or a mute option.

Placement and installation tips

For a countertop model, make sure the outlet is within reach and the unit isn't crammed against a wall in a way that blocks its vents. Give it a little breathing room so air can circulate.

For an over-the-range model, factor in the mounting height above the cooktop and whether it will vent outside or recirculate air back into the kitchen. Because installation is more permanent, it's worth measuring twice and, if you're unsure, asking an installer to confirm the fit.

Cleaning and upkeep

Interiors with a smooth, easy-wipe surface save time, and a removable turntable can go in the sink or dishwasher depending on the model. Wiping spills promptly keeps odors down and makes deep cleaning less of a chore. If a model has a vent hood, plan to clean or replace the grease filter periodically to keep airflow strong.

Bottom line

Match the format to your space first. A countertop microwave is the easiest, most flexible pick for most kitchens; an over-the-range microwave saves counter space and adds ventilation when you're set up for it; and a compact microwave fits tight quarters and secondary rooms. Once you've chosen the format, use capacity, wattage, and controls to narrow things down, and treat presets and sensor cooking as nice-to-have extras. Measure your space and your dishes before you buy, and you'll end up with a microwave that fits both your kitchen and the way you cook.
