How to Build Credit from Scratch (with No Credit History)

Quick answer: To build credit from scratch, open one starter account that reports to the credit bureaus — a secured credit card, a credit-builder loan, or authorized-user status on someone else's card — then pay on time and keep your balances low. A first credit score usually forms after about six months of activity. Building a good score (670+) takes longer, often a year or more.

This is general educational information, not personalized financial advice — your situation may differ. If you have no credit history at all, you're in very good company: the CFPB estimates about 26 million U.S. adults (roughly 1 in 10) are "credit invisible," meaning no score exists yet. Below you'll find the legitimate methods, how each one works, which to start with, and a realistic timeline.

What is a credit score, and why does it matter?

A credit score is a three-digit number lenders use to estimate how likely you are to repay borrowed money. The most widely used model is FICO, which runs from 300 to 850. Landlords, card issuers, auto lenders, and even some employers and insurers look at your credit profile, so a thin or empty file can mean higher deposits, higher rates, or outright denials.

Here's roughly how FICO groups the ranges (per Experian and major banks):

Score range FICO category
300–579 Poor
580–669 Fair
670–739 Good
740–799 Very Good
800–850 Exceptional

Most people aim for Good (670+) as a first target. One important myth to clear up: you don't start at zero or at 300. When you have no history, you simply have no score at all — the model can't calculate one until an account has reported for several months. The goal of everything below is to generate that first score, then nudge it upward.

The main ways to build credit from scratch

There are four legitimate, widely recommended starter tools. Experian, NerdWallet, CNBC Select, Citi, and the CFPB all converge on roughly this list:

  1. Secured credit card — a card backed by a refundable cash deposit.
  2. Credit-builder loan — a small loan where you "pay first, get the money after."
  3. Authorized-user status — getting added to a trusted person's existing card.
  4. Rent and bill reporting — newer tools that report on-time rent or utility payments.

You don't need all four. Most beginners start with one or two and add habits over time. Let's break down how each works.

Method 1 — Secured credit card

A secured credit card is the most common starting point. You put down a refundable cash deposit — often somewhere in the $200–$500 range — and that deposit typically becomes your credit limit. You then use the card for small everyday purchases and pay the balance in full and on time every month. The issuer reports that positive history to the bureaus, and a score begins to form.

Because the deposit lowers the issuer's risk, these cards are far easier to get approved for with no history. After a stretch of consistent on-time use, many issuers refund your deposit and/or "graduate" you to a regular unsecured card.

The honest trade-offs:

  • Pro: Easy approval, builds payment history and helps your utilization (more on that below), and gives you a reusable card.
  • Con: Requires cash upfront for the deposit, and some cards carry annual fees. Treat it like a debit card you pay off monthly — carrying a balance just costs you interest.

We won't name a "best" secured card or quote current fees and APRs here — those change constantly. Compare current terms directly at the issuer before applying.

Method 2 — Credit-builder loan

A credit-builder loan works backward compared to a normal loan. Instead of getting cash upfront, the lender places the borrowed amount — typically $300–$1,000 (sometimes up to roughly $3,000) — into a locked savings or escrow account. You make fixed monthly payments, usually over 6 to 24 months, and you receive the money only after you finish paying.

Every on-time payment is reported to the bureaus, so you build payment history and end up with a small lump of savings at the same time. CFPB research found that opening a credit-builder loan increased the likelihood of having a credit score by about 24% among people who didn't already have a loan.

The honest trade-offs:

  • Pro: Forces a savings habit, adds installment experience (which helps your "credit mix"), and is designed specifically for thin or no-file borrowers.
  • Con: You don't get access to the money until the end, and some loans carry interest or fees. It's a commitment, not a flexible tool.

Method 3 — Become an authorized user

The lowest-cost first step is being added as an authorized user on a trusted family member's or partner's existing credit card. When the issuer reports that account, its positive payment history can land on your credit report too — helping you establish credit without applying for your own card or putting down a deposit.

This only works under specific conditions, and it's worth being honest about them:

  • It helps most when the primary account is in good standing — on-time payments, low balances. A maxed-out or late account can actually hurt you.
  • The primary cardholder remains responsible for the debt, so it requires real trust on both sides.
  • Not every issuer reports authorized-user activity to the bureaus — check before assuming it'll help.

You don't even have to use the card (or carry it) for it to count. But don't oversell it to yourself: it's a helpful boost, not a guaranteed full credit profile on its own.

Method 4 — Rent and bill reporting (a newer option)

A growing set of services will report your on-time rent, utility, or even subscription payments to one or more credit bureaus. Since rent is often your biggest monthly payment, getting credit for it can help — especially if you're not ready for a card or loan yet.

Keep expectations measured: coverage varies by bureau and by which FICO/VantageScore version a lender uses, so the impact isn't universal. We're not naming specific products here; treat this as a supplement to the methods above, not a replacement.

What actually raises your score

Once an account is reporting, two habits do most of the heavy lifting. To see why, here's how FICO weights the five factors that make up your score (per myFICO):

FICO factor Approx. weight What it means for a beginner
Payment history ~35% Pay on time, every time — this is the single biggest lever
Amounts owed / utilization ~30% Keep balances low relative to your limits
Length of credit history ~15% Grows automatically with time — start early
New credit ~10% Don't open many accounts at once
Credit mix ~10% A blend (e.g., card + installment loan) helps a little

The two rules that matter most when you're starting out:

  1. Never miss a due date. Payment history is ~35% of your score, and a single late payment can set you back. Autopay the minimum (and pay the full balance manually) so a busy week never costs you.
  2. Keep your credit utilization low. This is the percentage of your available limit you're using. Aim to keep balances well under 30% of your limit — ideally under about 10%. On a $300 secured-card limit, that means keeping the balance under ~$30–$90 when it reports.

Length of credit history and credit mix mostly take care of themselves as time passes — which is exactly why starting now, even with one small account, beats waiting.

How long does it take to build credit?

Here's the realistic timeline, framed as ranges (no one can promise a specific number):

  • About 6 months: You generally need at least one account reporting for roughly six months before a FICO score can be generated — that's the jump from "no score" to having one.
  • A year or more: Building a genuinely good score (670+) takes longer — typically a year-plus of consistent on-time payments and low utilization.

You'll see commercial claims like "+50 to 100 points in 12 months." Treat those as illustrative, not guaranteed — results vary widely based on your starting point and your habits. There's no legitimate way to "fix" credit overnight, and anyone promising that is best avoided.

Secured card vs. credit-builder loan: which first?

This is one of the most common questions, and the honest answer is it depends on you. Both build payment history; they just emphasize different things.

Secured credit card Credit-builder loan
How you pay Use it, then pay it off monthly Fixed monthly payments toward a locked sum
Helps utilization? Yes (revolving credit) No (installment)
Adds credit mix? It's a revolving line Yes — installment experience
Forces savings? No Yes — you get the money at the end
Best if you want A reusable payment tool Discipline + a savings cushion

Many sources note that doing both — if you can comfortably afford it — is the fastest, most well-rounded path, because you cover utilization and credit mix at once. But that's a general framing, not a directive; pick what fits your budget and habits.

Check your progress for free

You can monitor your credit at no cost, and you should:

  • AnnualCreditReport.com is the only federally authorized source for free credit reports from all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion). Use it to confirm your accounts are reporting correctly.
  • Many banks and apps offer free credit-score tracking so you can watch your number form over time.

One reassurance: checking your own credit is a soft inquiry and does not hurt your score. Only applications you submit (hard inquiries) can ding it slightly. Pull your reports, make sure the positive history is showing up, and watch for errors.

FAQ

How do I build credit if I have no credit history?
Open one account that reports to the bureaus — a secured credit card, a credit-builder loan, or authorized-user status on a trusted person's card — then pay on time and keep balances low. A first score usually forms after about six months.

What is the fastest way to build credit?
There's no overnight fix. The fastest legitimate path is opening a reporting account right away, paying every bill on time, and keeping utilization very low (ideally under ~10%). Some people open both a secured card and a credit-builder loan to cover more score factors at once.

How long does it take to build credit from scratch?
You typically need about six months of activity before a FICO score can even be generated. Reaching a "good" score (670+) usually takes a year or more of on-time payments and low balances.

Is a secured card or a credit-builder loan better?
Neither is universally better. A secured card helps your utilization and gives you a reusable card; a credit-builder loan adds installment experience and forces savings. If you can afford both, doing both is the most well-rounded option.

What credit score do you start with?
You don't start at 300 or zero — you start with no score at all. A score can only be calculated once an account has reported for several months.

Does becoming an authorized user really build credit?
It can, if the primary account is in good standing and the issuer reports authorized-user activity to the bureaus. It's a low-cost head start, but not all issuers report it, and the primary cardholder still owns the debt.

The bottom line

Building credit from scratch isn't complicated — it just takes one good account and a little patience. Open a starter tool that reports to the bureaus, pay on time, and keep your balances low. A first score generally forms in about six months, and a good score follows with another year or so of steady habits.
