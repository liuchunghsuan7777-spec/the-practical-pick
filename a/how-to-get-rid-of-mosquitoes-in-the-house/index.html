How to Get Rid of Mosquitoes in the House (DEET vs Picaridin)

Quick answer: To get rid of mosquitoes in your house, do three things — (1) stop them getting in by repairing screens and keeping doors closed, (2) kill their nursery by emptying anything that holds standing water at least once a week, and (3) protect your sleep with a bed net, a fan, and an EPA-registered repellent (DEET or picaridin) on exposed skin.

It's the time of year when one mosquito in the bedroom can cost you a whole night of sleep. The good news is that most of the work is about prevention, not chasing bugs around with a rolled-up magazine.

This guide walks you through how to keep mosquitoes out of your house, how to stop them breeding indoors, how to clear your bedroom at night, and how to choose and safely use a repellent — including the popular DEET vs picaridin question and what's safe for kids and during pregnancy.

This is general information, not medical advice. Use any repellent according to the product label and EPA/CDC guidance. If you have a reaction to a bite or a product, talk to a clinician. Results vary from person to person.

1. Stop mosquitoes getting into your house

Catching mosquitoes is a losing game. Keeping them out is the win.

The two CDC basics are simple: "Install or repair and use window and door screens" and "Close doors, including garage doors." (Source: CDC, Mosquito Control at Home.) A screen with a small tear or a door propped open on a summer evening is an open invitation.


Common mosquito entry points to seal: a torn window screen, a gap under a door, and an open garage door.

Walk your home like a mosquito would:

  • Window and door screens — patch tears with screen repair tape and make sure the frame sits flush. Even a small gap lets them slip in.
  • Doors — keep exterior and garage doors closed; consider a self-closing hinge if a door tends to drift open.
  • Gaps and vents — check where screens have pulled away from the frame.

A quick note for English readers: some advice (often aimed at other regions) focuses on drains and floor traps. For most homes the bigger payoff is screens plus standing water outdoors, which is where the CDC points first.

2. Kill the breeding source: standing water

This is the step people skip, and it's the one that matters most. Mosquitoes don't need a pond — they need a puddle.

A female mosquito can lay hundreds of eggs in as little as a bottle-cap of standing water. (Source: Aptive, industry guidance — treat as general framing; the CDC is the anchor for removing standing water.) That's why a forgotten plant saucer can quietly become a mosquito factory.

The CDC's rule of thumb is weekly: "Once a week, empty and scrub, turn over, cover, or throw out any items that hold water" — planters, buckets, toys, tires, and trash containers — and tightly cover any water-storage containers. (Source: CDC, Mosquito Control at Home.)

Your weekly water sweep:

  • Tip out and scrub plant saucers, buckets, pet bowls, and kids' toys
  • Clear clogged gutters so rainwater drains instead of pooling
  • Cover or turn over anything you can't empty (rain barrels, wheelbarrows)
  • Refresh birdbaths and outdoor pet water regularly

If you've done all of this and still have mosquitoes indoors, an indoor insecticide is a last step, not a substitute. The CDC is clear: use one only after screens are fixed and containers are emptied, because "using only an indoor insecticide will not keep your home free of mosquitoes." (Source: CDC.)

3. How to get rid of mosquitoes in the bedroom at night

The bite that wakes you up at 3 a.m. is the worst one. Here, the most reliable tools are physical barriers — not chemicals.

Method How well it works
Bed net The most reliable physical barrier; treated nets add protection
Fan Real but limited — disrupts their flight and scatters your scent cues
Cover exposed skin Simple barrier — long sleeves/pants while you sleep
Repellent on skin Effective when an EPA-registered product is used as directed

A bed net is the surest way to sleep mosquito-free. For extra protection, a permethrin-treated, WHO-approved net helps — and you shouldn't wash the treatment out. (Source: The Healthy.)

Does a fan keep mosquitoes away? Partly, yes. Mosquitoes are weak fliers, so a fan's breeze disrupts their flight and disperses the carbon dioxide and body-scent cues they home in on. The effect is real but short-range and limited — a supplement, not a solution. (Source: Terminix; Ehrlich.)

Cover up. Long sleeves and pants are an easy barrier if you tend to get bitten at night. (Source: Tom's Guide.)

4. DEET vs picaridin vs other repellents

When it's time to put something on your skin, the rule is simple: use an EPA-registered repellent. Registration means the EPA has reviewed the product for safety and effectiveness when used as directed.

The EPA-registered active ingredients are DEET, picaridin (also called icaridin or KBR 3023), IR3535, oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), para-menthane-diol (PMD), and 2-undecanone. (Source: CDC, Preventing Mosquito Bites.)

Ingredient EPA-registered? Age rule (US) Notes
DEET Yes OK from 2 months; AAP: up to ~30% for kids The classic standard; can feel greasy and has an odor
Picaridin Yes OK from 2 months No odor, non-greasy, doesn't damage clothing/plastics
IR3535 Yes OK from 2 months Generally well tolerated
Oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE) / PMD Yes Not for children under 3 The one CDC-recognized "natural" option
2-undecanone Yes OK from 2 months Less common

So, DEET vs picaridin — which should you pick? The EPA has concluded that normal use of both DEET and picaridin "do not present a health concern." Studies show picaridin is at least as effective as DEET against mosquitoes (slightly better in some), and equally effective against ticks. (Source: VDCI; AMC, summarizing the EPA.)

Picaridin's practical edge is comfort: no chemical smell, non-greasy, and it won't damage clothing, plastics, or gear. (Source: AMC; REI.)

The honest takeaway: don't think of one as "safer" overall. The EPA considers both safe when used as directed — picaridin just has some practical advantages many people prefer.

5. How to apply repellent safely

Even a great product can irritate your skin or underperform if you use it wrong. These application rules come straight from the CDC.

Where and how to apply

  • Apply only to exposed skin and clothing — never under clothing.
  • Don't spray your face. Spray your hands first, then pat it on, avoiding eyes and mouth.
  • For children: spray it on your own hands and rub it on the child — never on the child's hands (which end up in their mouth and eyes).

Order and reapplication

  • Using sunscreen too? Apply sunscreen first, then repellent. (Source: CDC/AAP.)
  • Reapply according to the product label — higher DEET percentages last longer, but a higher percentage is not "stronger." (Source: AAP HealthyChildren.)
  • Wash it off with soap and water when you come back indoors.

The age rules worth memorizing:

  • Babies under 2 months: do not use any repellent. Use a net, clothing, and screened or shaded areas instead. (Source: CDC/AAP.)
  • Oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE) / PMD: do not use on children under 3 years. (Source: CDC/AAP.)
  • DEET, picaridin, IR3535, 2-undecanone: can be used on children from 2 months, following the label. (Source: CDC.)

Pregnant or breastfeeding? EPA-registered repellents are considered safe and effective when used as directed, and most studies do not find that DEET increases the risk of birth defects. (Source: MotherToBaby / NIH Bookshelf.)

If you notice itching, redness, a rash, or any reaction, stop using the product, wash the area, and talk to a clinician. Sensitivity varies from person to person.

6. Do natural mosquito repellents work?

Short answer: one does reliably, the rest are hit-or-miss.

Oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE) is the exception. It's the one "natural" option the CDC recognizes as a registered active ingredient — so it sits in a different category from generic essential oils. Just remember the under-3 restriction. (Source: CDC.)

Citronella, lavender, basil, essential-oil sprays, and "repellent plants" may help a little, but they're short-lasting and inconsistent — not a replacement for an EPA-registered repellent. (Source: Healthline.)

Popular DIY hacks — vinegar-sugar-dish-soap traps, garlic sprays, burning camphor — circulate widely but lack solid evidence that they work. Treat them as "popular, not proven." (Source: Homes & Gardens.)

⚠️ When to see a doctor

Most mosquito bites are just itchy and fade on their own. But get medical care if you notice any of these:

  • Signs of a serious allergic reaction — widespread hives, swelling of the lips, face, or throat, or trouble breathing. This is an emergency — call emergency services.
  • A bite that becomes hot, red, and swollen with fever — this can be a sign of a skin infection that may need treatment.
  • Fever, headache, or a rash after travel to an area with mosquito-borne diseases — mention the travel to your doctor.
  • A reaction to a repellent — stop the product, wash the skin, and seek care if it doesn't settle.

When in doubt, it's always reasonable to check with a clinician.

FAQ

Is DEET safe for kids?
Yes, when used as directed. The CDC says DEET can be used on children from 2 months of age, and the AAP considers products with up to about 30% DEET appropriate for children. Apply it to your own hands and rub it on the child, avoiding their hands, eyes, and mouth.

Can I use insect repellent while pregnant?
EPA-registered repellents are considered safe and effective during pregnancy and breastfeeding when used as directed, and most studies do not find DEET raises the risk of birth defects. (Source: MotherToBaby.) If you have concerns, ask your obstetric provider.

Does a fan really keep mosquitoes away?
Partly. A fan's breeze disrupts mosquitoes' flight and scatters the scent cues they track, so it helps near where you sleep. But the effect is limited and short-range — use it alongside a bed net and repellent, not instead of them.

Do natural or essential-oil repellents work?
Oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE) is CDC-recognized and works, with a no-under-3 rule. Most other essential oils and DIY traps are short-lasting or unproven — fine as extras, but not a substitute for an EPA-registered repellent.

Sunscreen or repellent first?
Sunscreen first, then repellent on top. (Source: CDC/AAP.)
