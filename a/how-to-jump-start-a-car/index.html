How to Jump Start a Car Safely: 7-Step Guide (2026)

Quick answer: To jump start a car, connect the red clamp to the dead battery's positive (+) terminal, the other red clamp to the good battery's positive (+), the black clamp to the good battery's negative (–), and the final black clamp to a piece of bare unpainted metal on the dead car (engine block or frame) — not the dead battery's negative terminal. Start the good car, wait about 2 minutes, then start the dead one.

That last black clamp going to bare metal instead of the battery terminal is the single most important safety step, and we'll explain exactly why below. But first, one quick check that can save you from a dangerous mistake.

Before You Touch Anything: When NOT to Jump Start

Most dead batteries are perfectly safe to jump. A few are not. Read this gate first.

Do not attempt a jump start — call roadside assistance (AAA or similar) instead — if:

  • The battery is cracked, bulging, swollen, or leaking fluid.
  • The battery is frozen (you may see ice, slush, or a distorted case after a hard freeze). Jumping a frozen battery can cause it to rupture.
  • You smell a strong rotten-egg (sulfur) odor near the battery — a sign of an internal fault.
  • You drive an electric vehicle (EV) or hybrid and are unsure of the procedure. These have a small 12V battery that can usually be jumped, but you must never connect cables to the high-voltage pack. Follow the exact jump-start points in your owner's manual, and when in doubt, call for service.

If any of those apply, stop here. The cost of a tow is far smaller than the cost of a battery rupture, an acid burn, or a damaged high-voltage system.

Still good to go? Then run through the safety setup next.

Safety Setup: 60 Seconds That Prevent Sparks and Injury

Before a single clamp touches metal:

  • Turn both vehicles completely off and remove the keys (or power off push-start systems).
  • Set the parking brakes on both cars and put them in Park (or neutral for manuals).
  • Switch off all accessories — headlights, radio, climate control, phone chargers — on both vehicles.
  • Position the cars close but not touching. Metal-to-metal contact between the two vehicles can create a short.
  • Wear eye protection if you have it. You're working near a battery that can spark and, in rare cases, vent gas.
  • Inspect the cables. Frayed, cracked, or melted-looking jumper cables should not be used.

Step-by-Step: How to Jump Start a Car With Cables (7 Steps)

This is the verified, safe sequence. Follow it in order. The colors and order are not interchangeable.

  1. Position and prep. Park the donor car close to the dead car (not touching), both engines off, brakes set, accessories off.
  2. Red to dead (+). Clamp one red (positive) clamp onto the positive (+) terminal of the dead battery. The positive terminal is usually marked + and often has a red cover.
  3. Red to good (+). Clamp the other red clamp onto the positive (+) terminal of the good (donor) battery.
  4. Black to good (–). Clamp one black (negative) clamp onto the negative (–) terminal of the good battery.
  5. Black to bare metal on the dead car. Clamp the final black clamp onto a clean, unpainted metal surface on the dead car — a bolt on the engine block or a metal part of the frame, at least about a foot away from the battery. Do not clamp it to the dead battery's negative terminal. (See the warning box below — this prevents an explosion risk.)
  6. Start the good car, then wait. Start the donor vehicle and let it idle for about 2 minutes so it can feed charge into the dead battery.
  7. Start the dead car. Try to start the previously dead car. If it cranks to life, leave it running and move to disconnecting.

Why the last black clamp goes to bare metal — not the battery

A discharged battery can release small amounts of hydrogen gas, which is flammable. Making that final connection always produces a tiny spark. If that spark happens right at the battery's negative terminal, it can ignite the hydrogen and, in a worst case, cause the battery to rupture and spray sulfuric acid. Clamping to bare metal a foot away moves the spark away from the gas. This is not optional — it's the core safety rule of jump starting.

If the car starts: disconnect in reverse order

Remove the clamps in the exact reverse of how you attached them, and don't let the clamps touch each other or any metal while a cable is still live:

  1. Black clamp off the bare metal of the revived car.
  2. Black clamp off the donor battery's negative terminal.
  3. Red clamp off the donor battery's positive terminal.
  4. Red clamp off the revived battery's positive terminal.

Then drive the revived car for at least 15–20 minutes (or idle it that long) so the alternator can recharge the battery. If you shut it off too soon, it may not restart.

No Second Car? How to Use a Portable Jump Starter

A portable jump starter (also called a jump box or jump pack) has its own internal battery, so you don't need a donor vehicle. The general process:

  1. Make sure the jump pack is charged (check it every couple of months — a dead jump pack is useless in an emergency).
  2. With your car off, clamp the pack's red clamp to the battery's positive (+) terminal.
  3. Clamp the black clamp to bare unpainted metal on your car (same rule as cables — not the negative terminal), unless your unit's instructions specify otherwise.
  4. Power on the unit and start the car. Most units warn you not to crank for more than a few seconds at a time.
  5. Disconnect black first, then red, and drive 15–20 minutes to recharge.

Peak amps vs. cranking amps — the spec that actually matters. Marketing loves to print a giant peak amps number (a brief burst). What does the real work of turning your starter is the cranking amps (sustained output). Cheaper units sometimes inflate the peak figure while quietly listing modest cranking amps, so read both.

As a rough, general guideline only: many sources suggest a higher-output unit for larger engines (big trucks, V8 SUVs) and that a smaller car needs less. Rather than chase a single magic number, match the jump starter's rated amps to your vehicle's engine size and what your owner's manual recommends. If you tow, drive a diesel, or live somewhere very cold, size up.

A few extra things worth checking before you buy:

  • Clamps with reverse-polarity and spark protection (forgives a wrong connection).
  • A built-in light for nighttime roadside use.
  • USB ports so it doubles as a phone power bank.
  • A rated number of jumps per charge — and how long it holds a charge in storage.

After the Jump: Troubleshooting

Car won't start even after a jump? A few likely reasons:

  • The battery is beyond recovery. A very old or deeply discharged battery may not hold a charge anymore and needs replacing.
  • Loose or corroded connections. Make sure the clamps have clean, tight metal contact. Corrosion (white/blue crust) on the terminals blocks current.
  • The problem isn't the battery. A failing alternator, starter, or another electrical fault won't be fixed by a jump. If a freshly jumped car dies again soon after, have the charging system tested.

Will driving recharge the battery? Partly. The alternator recharges the battery while you drive, so a 15–20 minute drive helps. But a battery that was drained flat may need a proper overnight charge — or replacement — to fully recover.

Battery died again the next morning? That points to a battery near the end of its life or a "parasitic" drain (something staying powered with the car off). Time for a battery test at an auto-parts store or shop.

FAQ

Do you connect the positive or negative first?
Connect positive (red) first — red to the dead battery's positive, then red to the good battery's positive. Negatives come after, and the very last clamp (black) goes to bare metal on the dead car, not its battery terminal.

Can you jump start a car by yourself?
Yes — with a portable jump starter you don't need anyone else. With jumper cables you need a second running vehicle, so you'd need a helper or a willing stranger.

Why don't you connect the last clamp to the negative terminal?
Because that final connection sparks, and a discharged battery can release flammable hydrogen gas. Sparking at bare metal a foot away keeps the spark away from the gas and greatly reduces the risk of a battery rupture or acid spray.

How long does it take to jump start a car?
Usually just a few minutes of setup plus about 2 minutes of letting the donor car charge the dead battery. Then plan to drive 15–20 minutes afterward so the alternator can recharge it.

Can a completely dead battery be jumped?
Often yes, if the battery is otherwise healthy — let the donor car run a little longer before trying. But a battery that's failed internally, is cracked/bulging, or is frozen should not be jumped; replace it or call for service.

Will the battery be fine after one jump?
A single jump from an occasional drain (lights left on) is usually fine after a good recharge. Repeated jumps in a short time mean the battery or charging system needs to be checked.

Final Takeaway

Jump starting a car is genuinely simple once you lock in two rules: red goes to positives, the last black clamp goes to bare metal on the dead car — never its negative terminal, and you never jump a damaged, frozen, or high-voltage battery. Keep a set of jumper cables or a charged portable jump starter in your trunk, follow the order above, and a dead battery becomes a five-minute fix instead of a stranded afternoon.
