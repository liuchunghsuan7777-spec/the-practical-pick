Hawaiian Burger with Grilled Pineapple: Easy Recipe & Tips

A Hawaiian burger is a beef patty topped with grilled pineapple, melty cheese, and teriyaki sauce — sweet-meets-savory in one bite. The grilled pineapple is the star: charring it caramelizes the natural sugars and gives you that sticky, smoky-sweet edge against a juicy beef patty.

In this guide you'll get the full picture: what a Hawaiian burger actually is, a simple step-by-step recipe, how to grill the pineapple so it doesn't go mushy, the best cheese and sauce to use, and the honest answer to the "does pineapple belong on a burger?" debate. There's also a quick, no-hype note on whether grilled pineapple is good for you.

The Hawaiian burger build, layer by layer: brioche bun, grilled pineapple, teriyaki, melted Swiss, beef patty, and lettuce.

What is a Hawaiian burger?

A Hawaiian burger is a burger built around the sweet-and-savory combo of grilled pineapple plus a savory patty and cheese, usually finished with teriyaki sauce. Lettuce, red onion, and bacon are common add-ons. (Taste of Home; Dole; Food Network)

The patty doesn't have to be beef. You'll see the same build done with:

  • Beef — the classic, richest version
  • Chicken or turkey — lighter; many people think pineapple pairs even better here
  • Salmon — a popular lighter twist with teriyaki

The "Hawaiian" name is really about the pineapple-and-teriyaki flavor profile rather than any single fixed recipe, so treat the version below as a reliable starting point you can adapt.

  • Toppings to taste — lettuce, red onion, bacon
  • Salt and pepper

How to make it (step by step)

1. Form the patties

Keep the beef cold and mix it as little as possible — about 30 to 40 seconds. Overworking the meat makes patties dense and tough. Shape four patties slightly wider than your buns (they shrink as they cook) and press a small dimple in the center so they stay flat. Season with salt and pepper just before cooking. (Taste of Home; Chelsea's Messy Apron)

2. Grill the pineapple — the key step

This is what separates a Hawaiian burger from a regular burger with a sad cold ring on top.

  • Pat the pineapple dry first. Surface moisture steams instead of caramelizing, so blotting the rings helps them char. (Chelsea's Messy Apron)
  • Grill until caramelized and tender, about 3 minutes per side. Grilling concentrates the sugars and flavor. You want clear grill marks and softened, glossy fruit. (Bake It With Love; McCormick)
  • Canned works fine — just choose juice-packed, not syrup-packed, and save the juice for the sauce or a marinade. (Chelsea's Messy Apron; Taste of Home)

Grill the pineapple about 3 minutes per side — pat it dry first for clear, caramelized grill marks.

3. Cook the patties

Grill over medium-high, around 450–475°F (230–245°C), flipping once. In the last minute, lay a slice of cheese on each patty and let it melt. (Taste of Home; Chelsea's Messy Apron; Bake It With Love)

4. Assemble

Toast the buns if you like. Build from the bottom: bun, lettuce, patty with melted cheese, a grilled pineapple ring, a drizzle of teriyaki, onion, top bun. Serve right away while everything's warm.

Best cheese and sauce

Cheese

Swiss is the go-to — it melts smoothly and its mild nuttiness doesn't fight the pineapple. Havarti is another great melter. Here's a quick comparison so you can match it to your taste. (Chelsea's Messy Apron; Bake It With Love; Carlsbad Cravings)

Cheese Flavor Best if you want
Swiss Mild, nutty, melts smoothly The classic, balanced choice
Havarti Buttery, creamy melt Extra-melty and mellow
Pepper jack Mild heat A sweet-and-spicy contrast
Cheddar Sharp, tangy More bite against the pineapple
Mozzarella Mild, creamy, stretchy Soft, gentle creaminess

Sauce

Teriyaki is the standard partner for sweet pineapple and savory beef. You can buy it, or make a quick batch: simmer soy sauce + pineapple juice + brown sugar + grated ginger for about 15–18 minutes, then thicken with a cornstarch slurry. (Chelsea's Messy Apron; WellPlated)

If teriyaki isn't your thing, a smoky BBQ sauce also works well with grilled pineapple.

Does pineapple belong on a burger? The debate

Let's be honest: pineapple on a burger is divisive — though usually less heated than the pineapple-on-pizza argument. Opinions tend to split between "love both, just not together" and "a small amount is actually great." (The Daily Meal; Quora community thread)

A practical takeaway from the people who do like it: pineapple often shines best on chicken, turkey, or salmon with BBQ or teriyaki, rather than on a beef patty with ketchup. The teriyaki-and-grilled approach gives the sweetness something savory and umami to lean on, which is exactly why the Hawaiian build works for so many people. If you're skeptical, start with one thin grilled ring and judge from there.

Fun fact: the McDonald's "Hula Burger" flop

Pineapple-on-a-bun has a famous failure. In the early 1960s, McDonald's founder Ray Kroc introduced the Hula Burger — grilled pineapple and cheese on a bun — aimed at customers skipping meat on Fridays. In a head-to-head test it reportedly lost badly to the Filet-O-Fish (the often-cited figures are roughly 6 Hula Burgers to 350 fish sandwiches). (Mashed)

Pineapple-topped burgers later found a real home in Australia, where pineapple is a standard "Aussie burger" topping. (Mashed) So the idea wasn't wrong — it just needed the right execution and the right crowd.

The exact "6 vs 350" numbers come from food media rather than a primary McDonald's source, so treat them as reported, not gospel.

Is grilled pineapple healthy? A quick note

Pineapple itself is genuinely nutritious. One cup (about 165 g) has roughly 75 calories, around a third of the Daily Value for vitamin C, over 100% DV of manganese, about 10% DV fiber, plus some B vitamins. (Cleveland Clinic)

Pineapple is also the only known food that contains bromelain, an enzyme studied for anti-inflammatory and digestive effects. (Cleveland Clinic; NIH/NCCIH)

A few honest caveats, though:

  • Bromelain's benefits are mostly studied as concentrated supplements, and large human studies are limited — eating pineapple on a burger is not a proven health treatment.
  • Grilling caramelizes the fruit's sugars, and a cheeseburger is still a cheeseburger. The pineapple doesn't turn it into a health food.

This is general information, not medical advice. If you have specific dietary concerns, talk to a qualified professional.

Frequently asked questions

Can I use canned pineapple?
Yes. Choose rings packed in juice, not syrup, pat them dry, and grill as usual. Save the juice for the sauce or a marinade. (Chelsea's Messy Apron; Taste of Home)

What's the best cheese for a Hawaiian burger?
Swiss is the classic for its smooth melt and mild, nutty flavor. Havarti is a close second; pepper jack adds a little heat. (Chelsea's Messy Apron; Carlsbad Cravings)

Do I have to grill the pineapple?
You don't have to, but it's worth it. Grilling caramelizes the sugars and concentrates the flavor — about 3 minutes per side is the sweet spot. (Bake It With Love; McCormick)

What sauce goes on a Hawaiian burger?
Teriyaki is the standard. A smoky BBQ sauce is a solid alternative if you'd rather skip the sweetness of teriyaki. (Chelsea's Messy Apron; WellPlated)

Is pineapple on a burger weird?
It's divisive, but plenty of people love it — especially grilled pineapple with teriyaki on chicken, turkey, or salmon. Try one thin ring before you decide. (The Daily Meal)

Can I make it with chicken instead of beef?
Absolutely. Chicken (or turkey, or salmon) is a popular lighter base, and many people feel pineapple pairs even better with it than with beef. (Taste of Home; Dole)
