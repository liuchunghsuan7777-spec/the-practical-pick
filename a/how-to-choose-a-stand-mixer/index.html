How to Choose a Stand Mixer (vs Hand Mixer)

Bowl size, wattage, and attachments — decide between a stand mixer and a hand mixer for your baking.

Mixing by hand works until it doesn't, and the right machine turns weeknight baking from a chore into something you actually look forward to. This guide walks through the choices that matter so you can match a mixer to the way you really cook.

Stand mixer vs. hand mixer: who needs which

The first fork in the road is deciding between a stand mixer and a hand mixer, and it comes down to volume and ambition. A hand mixer is light, stores in a drawer, and handles the everyday jobs: whipping cream, beating egg whites, creaming butter and sugar for a single batch of cookies, or mashing potatoes. You hold it, you guide it, and you clean two beaters when you're done. For occasional bakers and small kitchens, that's often all you need.

A stand mixer earns its counter space when you bake often or in bigger batches. Because the motor and bowl do the work, your hands are free to add ingredients, and the machine can knead stiff bread dough or run a double batch of frosting without you hovering over it. If you regularly make yeast breads, pizza dough, or large quantities of anything, the hands-free power is hard to give up. Many people end up owning both: the hand mixer for quick, light tasks and the stand mixer for the heavy, time-consuming work.

What to look for

Once you've settled on a stand mixer, a handful of specs separate a model you'll love from one that frustrates you.

Bowl capacity. Think in terms of how many cookie batches or loaves you make at once. A smaller bowl is fine for singles and couples, while a larger bowl saves you from mixing in shifts when you bake for a crowd or double recipes for the freezer. Just remember that a bigger bowl doesn't whip small amounts well, since the beater may not reach a thin layer at the bottom.

Wattage and motor for dough. Higher power and a sturdy motor matter most if you knead bread. Stiff, low-hydration doughs put real strain on a machine, and an underpowered one will bog down, walk across the counter, or overheat. If you mostly whip and cream lighter mixtures, you can prioritize other features over raw power.

Tilt-head vs. bowl-lift. Tilt-head models let you flip the top back to swap attachments and scrape the bowl, which feels intuitive and works well for most home baking. Bowl-lift models raise the bowl to the beater on a sturdy frame and tend to be more stable under heavy dough loads, though they usually need more clearance and a taller footprint.

Included attachments. Most machines come with a few core tools: a flat beater for general mixing, a wire whisk for aerating, and a dough hook for kneading. Confirm which pieces are in the box and whether they're dishwasher-safe, since coated versus stainless pieces clean up differently.

Attachment hub. This is the quietly important one. Many stand mixers include a front power hub that drives optional accessories, turning the base into a small kitchen system. Through that hub you can add stand mixer attachments like pasta rollers, meat grinders, and vegetable slicers. If expanding down the road appeals to you, check that the model you're eyeing has a compatible hub before you buy.

Footprint and storage. A stand mixer is heavy and tall, so measure your counter and the cabinet clearance above it, especially for tilt-head models that need room to open. If you plan to leave it out, make sure the spot works; if you'll store it, be honest about whether you'll actually haul it out often.

Weighing your real habits

Specs only matter in the context of how you cook. Be realistic about frequency: a powerful stand mixer that lives in a closet may see less use than a hand mixer that's always within reach. Consider who's lifting it, how much you store, and whether you're buying for the baking you do now or the baking you hope to grow into. It's fine to start simple and upgrade later once your habits are clear.

Also think about noise, cleanup, and the number of parts you're willing to wash. A hand mixer wins on simplicity every time, while a stand mixer trades a little convenience for capacity and consistency. Neither is objectively better; they solve different problems.

Bottom line

Choose a hand mixer if your baking is occasional, light, and best served by something quick to grab and easy to stash. Choose a stand mixer if you bake often, knead dough, or work in big batches and want hands-free power, and weigh bowl capacity, motor strength, tilt-head versus bowl-lift, and the attachment hub against your kitchen space. Match the tool to your real routine, and you'll reach for it far more than you expected.
