1. Documents to sort before you pack

The single most stressful airport moment usually comes down to paperwork, not packing. Sort these first.

Five documents to sort before you pack — passport validity, visa/ETIAS, tickets, insurance, and STEP.

  • Passport validity (the "6-month rule"). Many countries require your passport to be valid at least 6 months beyond your travel dates, and an airline can deny boarding if you don't meet it. There are exceptions: the Schengen Area in Europe asks for validity of 3 months beyond your planned departure, and places like the UK, Ireland, and Japan generally just need it valid through your stay. The 6-month rule also doesn't apply to a U.S. citizen re-entering the U.S. If you're cutting it close, renew early (Sources: U.S. State Department, travel.state.gov; U.S. Customs and Border Protection, "Six-Month Validity Update").

  • Visa or ETIAS. Requirements vary by destination and your nationality. As of early 2026, U.S. citizens still travel visa-free to Europe for trips under 90 days. The new ETIAS — a travel authorization, not a visa (about €20, valid roughly 3 years) — is scheduled to launch in late 2026 for 30 European countries; the UK and Ireland are not part of ETIAS. Always confirm entry rules for your specific destination before you book (Sources: European Union, travel-europe.europa.eu; U.S. State Department).

  • Travel insurance. A good policy covers trip cancellation, lost luggage, and medical care abroad. It's commonly recommended to buy it within about 15 days of your first trip payment to qualify for extra benefits, but coverage and exclusions vary by policy, so read the fine print (Source: general travel guidance — frame as commonly recommended, not guaranteed).

  • Enroll in STEP. The Smart Traveler Enrollment Program is a free U.S. State Department service that sends you safety alerts from the nearest U.S. embassy and lets them reach you in an emergency (Source: U.S. State Department, travel.state.gov).

  • Make copies of everything. Keep both paper copies and phone photos of your passport, tickets, insurance, and reservations in case anything is lost or stolen (Source: U.S. State Department checklist).

Tip: Store one set of copies separately from the originals — for example, paper copies in a different bag plus photos in your phone or cloud.

3. Carry-on vs. checked: the rules that trip people up

The two things most likely to get pulled at security are liquids and power banks. Get these right and screening is easy.

The two carry-on rules that trip people up — TSA 3-1-1 liquids and power-bank placement.

  • The TSA 3-1-1 liquids rule. Liquids, gels, aerosols, creams, and pastes in your carry-on must be in containers of 3.4 oz (100 ml) or less, all fitting in one quart-size (about 1 liter) clear zip-top bag, one bag per passenger. TSA cares about the container size, not how full it is — a 6 oz bottle is barred even if it's nearly empty. This federal standard is unchanged in 2026. (Many U.S. airports now use 3D CT scanners, so you may not need to remove the bag, but the size limit still applies.) (Source: TSA, tsa.gov).

  • Medically necessary liquids and baby formula may exceed 3.4 oz, but you should declare them to officers for inspection at screening (Source: TSA).

  • Power banks and spare batteries — carry-on only. Power banks and spare lithium batteries must travel in your carry-on, never in checked baggage. By capacity: up to 100 Wh is allowed, 101–160 Wh needs airline approval, and over 160 Wh is forbidden. Protect the terminals from short-circuiting (keep them in retail packaging, tape the terminals, or use a pouch). Airlines may set stricter limits on quantity, so check your carrier (Sources: FAA PackSafe, faa.gov; TSA Power Banks).

Carry-on and checked rules change often and differ by airline and country. Remember the durable basics — power banks in carry-on only, liquids 3.4 oz — and confirm exact figures and quantities with your airline and TSA/FAA shortly before departure.

4. Adapters, power, money & connectivity

Adapter vs. voltage converter

Outlets and voltage differ by country, and two devices often get confused. A plug adapter changes the shape of the plug. A voltage converter changes the voltage itself. They are not the same thing.

  • If a device charger is labeled "INPUT 100–240V" (dual voltage) — which is true for most phone, laptop, tablet, and camera chargers — you only need a plug adapter.
  • Single-voltage items (many hair dryers, flat irons, and travel irons) may need a converter or a dual-voltage travel version.
  • Most travelers carrying only a phone, laptop, tablet, and camera don't need a converter at all. Just confirm the plug type and voltage for your destination (Sources: World Standards, worldstandards.eu; Ceptics).

5. Traveling with medication

Medications are mostly about procedure. This section doesn't cover what to take or how much — for that, ask your doctor or pharmacist about your own medications.

Traveling with medication is about procedure — carry-on, labeled packaging, declare liquids, check the destination.

  • Keep prescriptions in your carry-on (not checked, so they can't get lost), ideally in their original, labeled packaging, and bring enough for the whole trip (Source: TSA; U.S. State Department).
  • Liquid medications over 3.4 oz are allowed, but you should declare them at screening for inspection (Source: TSA).
  • Some medications that are legal in the U.S. are restricted or illegal elsewhere. Check the destination country's rules (and embassy guidance) before you go, and carry a doctor's note for controlled medications (Source: U.S. State Department).

This is general procedural information, not medical advice and not a substitute for diagnosis or a prescription. If you take regular medication or have a health condition, talk to your doctor or pharmacist before you travel and confirm your destination's import rules.

6. Commonly forgotten items & loss prep

You packed the big stuff — and then realize at the gate that you left something small but important behind. These are the usual suspects.

  • Travel adapter — buying one abroad can be pricey or hard to find.
  • Power bank — even if you packed it, don't forget it must go in your carry-on (not checked), or you'll be pulling it out at security.
  • Passport copies and passport photos — for loss or emergency reissue.
  • Chargers and cables, travel-size toiletries (≤3.4 oz), and a light rain jacket.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

Is my passport valid if it expires in 5 months?
Possibly not, depending on where you're going. Many countries require at least 6 months of validity beyond your travel dates, and you can be denied boarding or entry without it. The Schengen Area asks for 3 months, and some countries just need validity through your stay. If you're under 6 months, renew early and confirm your destination's requirement on travel.state.gov.

Can I put a power bank in checked luggage?
No. Power banks and spare lithium batteries must go in your carry-on only, never in checked baggage, for safety reasons. Up to 100 Wh is allowed; 101–160 Wh needs airline approval; over 160 Wh is not permitted. Check your airline for any quantity limits.

How many ounces of liquid can I bring in a carry-on?
Each container must be 3.4 oz (100 ml) or less, and they all have to fit in one quart-size clear zip-top bag — that's the "3-1-1" rule. Larger containers go in checked baggage. Medically necessary liquids can exceed the limit if you declare them at screening.

Do U.S. citizens need a visa for Europe in 2026?
Not yet. As of early 2026, U.S. citizens can travel visa-free to Europe for stays under 90 days. The ETIAS travel authorization is scheduled to launch in late 2026; it's an online authorization, not a visa. Always confirm your destination's current entry rules before you go.

Do I need a voltage converter?
Usually not. If your charger says "INPUT 100–240V" (most phone, laptop, and camera chargers do), you only need a plug adapter. You'd only need a converter for single-voltage devices like some hair dryers or flat irons.

The bottom line

International travel prep really comes down to three things:

  • Documents first — passport valid well beyond your trip (many places want 6 months), visa or ETIAS, and travel insurance.
  • Pack by category — using the six-category checklist means far fewer forgotten items.
  • Know the airport basics — liquids 3.4 oz in one quart bag, and power banks in your carry-on only.

Rules and numbers change, so make it a habit to double-check the official sources before you fly. Do that, and the airport stops being the stressful part. Have a safe, easy trip.
