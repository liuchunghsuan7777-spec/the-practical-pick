Why Does My Ankle Keep Rolling? Chronic Instability & Fixes

Quick answer: If your ankle keeps rolling or giving way long after a sprain, you may have chronic ankle instability — often a stretched-out ligament that won't tighten back up on its own. The good news is that it's very treatable, and most people start with physical therapy rather than surgery.

You sprained your ankle once, years ago maybe, and it never really felt the same. Now the same ankle turns on a curb, on grass, on a flat sidewalk. You catch yourself bracing on stairs and uneven ground. If that sounds familiar, you're not just "clumsy" or stuck with a "weak ankle" — there may be a real, fixable problem underneath.

This article walks you through what chronic ankle instability is, why it happens, how doctors diagnose it (including the stress X-ray and MRI), what you can do at home first, and when surgery like the Broström procedure comes into the picture. This is general health information, not a diagnosis. The red-flag section near the end tells you when to get checked right away.

What is chronic ankle instability, and why does it happen?

Your ankle has two main ligaments on the outer side that keep it from rolling inward: the anterior talofibular ligament (ATFL) and the calcaneofibular ligament (CFL). (There's a third, the posterior talofibular ligament, but it's rarely injured.) Ligaments are the tough, rope-like bands that connect bone to bone. The ATFL is the weakest of the group and the one that usually tears first when you sprain your ankle.

About 90% of ankle sprains are inversion injuries — the foot rolls inward and stretches or tears those outer ligaments (Source: AAOS OrthoInfo).

Here's the catch with ligaments: once they stretch out, they don't always snap back to their original length, a bit like a rubber band that's been pulled too far. If that first sprain doesn't get enough rest, or the ligament heals in a loose, lengthened position, the ankle stays a little wobbly. After that, it doesn't take much — even normal walking — to make it roll again. When that keeps happening, it's called chronic ankle instability (Source: Foot Health Facts, American College of Foot and Ankle Surgeons; Mayo Clinic Health System).

This is more common than you'd think. Roughly 20–40% of people who sprain an ankle (about 1 in 3) go on to develop chronic instability (Source: OrthoVirginia). And repeated sprains can lead to longer-term problems including ongoing pain, instability, and even arthritis (Source: AAOS OrthoInfo).

The lateral ankle ligaments most often injured when the ankle rolls inward — the ATFL is the weakest and tears first.

Functional vs. mechanical instability (this decides your treatment)

Not all "wobbly ankles" are the same. Doctors split chronic ankle instability into two types, and the difference is what determines whether you'll need surgery (Source: Foot Health Facts, ACFAS).

Functional instability Mechanical instability
What's wrong The ligaments are relatively intact, but the muscles and your sense of balance (proprioception) are weak The ligaments are actually stretched or torn, so the joint is structurally loose
How it feels The ankle "feels" unstable The ankle is physically loose
Usual treatment Physical therapy, balance training, bracing — often no surgery May need surgery if rehab doesn't hold it

In plain terms: is it a muscle/balance problem or a stretched-ligament problem? Many people have a mix of both. That's exactly why a proper exam matters — the answer points you toward rehab, surgery, or some of each.

Symptoms and a quick self-check

You might have chronic ankle instability if several of these sound like you (Source: Foot Health Facts; Tampa General Hospital):

  • Your ankle keeps turning or rolling, especially on uneven ground or during sport
  • Persistent pain on the outer side of the ankle
  • Swelling and stiffness that come and go
  • A feeling that the ankle is going to "give out" under you
  • Low confidence walking on trails, curbs, or stairs

This is a self-awareness checklist, not a diagnosis. Plenty of other conditions (like peroneal tendon problems) can feel similar, so the only way to know is to get the ankle examined.

How chronic ankle instability is diagnosed

Diagnosing instability isn't done with a single picture. Doctors usually go step by step.

1) Physical exam first. The clinician gently moves and pulls on your ankle to feel how much it shifts compared with your healthy side (Source: AAOS OrthoInfo). A loose joint feels different in skilled hands.

2) Stress X-ray. This is a regular X-ray taken while a small, controlled force is applied to the ankle — bending the foot inward or pulling it forward. A loose ligament lets the joint open up more than a normal one would, like a worn-out hinge that rattles when you wiggle it. Comparing both ankles side by side shows clearly how much extra the injured one opens (Source: AAOS OrthoInfo).

On a stress X-ray, an unstable ankle tilts open more than the healthy side. Schematic concept, not an actual X-ray.

3) MRI. An MRI shows whether the ATFL is actually torn or thinned, and — importantly — whether anything else got injured along with it. In particular, chronic instability can come with cartilage damage (more on that next).

The cartilage complication: osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT)

When an ankle rolls over and over, the cartilage inside can take a beating too. The talus is the bone that sits between your shin bones and your heel bone, and the cartilage capping it can get chipped or damaged. That injury is called an osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT) (Source: Massachusetts General Hospital; HSS).

The clues are easy to miss because they're nonspecific: pain, swelling, stiffness, and a mechanical catching or locking sensation when you walk — like something is briefly jamming inside the joint. If those show up alongside instability, your doctor will likely look harder.

MRI is the gold standard for diagnosing an OLT, and it doubles as the test that confirms ligament damage at the same time (Source: MGH; HSS). This is one big reason chronic instability is worth taking seriously rather than shrugging off as a "trick ankle."

Try this first: home and conservative management

For most people — and almost everyone with the functional type — treatment starts without surgery. A focused rehab program can genuinely change how stable your ankle feels (Source: Foot Health Facts, ACFAS):

  • Peroneal strengthening: the muscles on the outer side of your lower leg that help stop the ankle from rolling
  • Balance and proprioception training: single-leg balance work and wobble-board drills to retrain your ankle's sense of position
  • Bracing: a lace-up brace for sport or uneven ground
  • Footwear: supportive shoes; being cautious on heels and very uneven terrain

These may help, and results vary from person to person. A physical therapist or your clinician can tailor a program to you — don't start aggressive exercises on a swollen, painful ankle without guidance.

When is surgery considered — and what is the Broström procedure?

First, the important part: not every unstable ankle needs surgery. Surgery usually enters the conversation only after about 3–6 months of proper conservative care hasn't worked, the ankle keeps giving way, and the exam and imaging show real mechanical (structural) instability. Leaving true instability untreated can raise the risk of cartilage damage and arthritis over time, which is part of why doctors don't ignore it (Source: PMC current-concepts review).

The most established operation is the Broström procedure (you'll also see it written "modified Broström" or "Broström-Gould," and often spelled Brostrom without the accent). It's an anatomic repair — meaning the surgeon repairs your own ligaments rather than replacing them. It's considered the gold standard, with better function and less later arthritis than older non-anatomic techniques (Source: PMC review).

Here's roughly what happens:

  • The surgeon tightens and reattaches your stretched ATFL and CFL to the fibula (the outer ankle bone), usually with small suture anchors — tiny anchors set into the bone that hold the stitches. Think of taking a stretched-out rope, shortening it, and tying it back snug.
  • The Gould modification adds a flap of nearby tissue (the inferior extensor retinaculum) over the repair for extra reinforcement (Source: Johns Hopkins Medicine).

A few variations you might hear about:

  • Arthroscopic vs. open. Arthroscopic repair uses a camera and small "keyhole" portals instead of one longer incision. Its advantages are less surgical trauma, faster early recovery, and the ability to fix problems inside the joint (like an OLT) at the same time. The trade-off is a steeper learning curve for the surgeon (Source: technique overview, michiganfootdoctors). It isn't automatically "better" — final long-term results are similar.
  • Suture-tape / "internal brace" augmentation. This adds a strong synthetic tape over the repaired ligament — essentially a seatbelt that shares the load while your own ligament heals and can allow earlier weight-bearing. Studies show outcomes and complication rates comparable to standard Broström repair — it's a safe option, not a guaranteed upgrade (Source: PubMed; Arthroscopy Journal systematic review).

How well does it work — and what are the limits? About 91% of patients report good-to-excellent results, and over 90% no longer feel the ankle "giving way" (Source: Johns Hopkins Medicine). That's encouraging, but to be honest about it: a review of multiple studies found a roughly 11% failure rate (range about 5.7–28.5%), meaning instability can come back (Source: PMC review). Like any surgery, it also carries general risks such as wound-healing problems, nerve irritation, and blood clots (DVT). Surgery is a good tool — not a guarantee.

Recovery: what to expect after Broström surgery

Recovery happens in stages, and the timeline below is a general guide, not a promise. Broström repair is often done as an outpatient (same-day) procedure (Source: Johns Hopkins Medicine; MGH Broström rehab protocol):

  • ~First 2 weeks: non-weight-bearing in a splint, on crutches, while the repair starts to heal
  • Next phase: a walking boot with gradually increasing weight on the foot
  • Around 4 weeks: transition to a lace-up brace and start formal physical therapy
  • Around 12 weeks: return to full sport for many people

Overall, plan on a minimum of 3–6 months before things feel solid. Your real timeline depends on your age, the quality of your ligament tissue, whether cartilage was also damaged, and how consistently you do your rehab. Your surgeon sets your schedule — don't rush back based on a number you read online.

Red flags: when to see a doctor right away

Most ankle problems can wait for a routine appointment, but get medical care promptly if any of these happen (Source: AAOS OrthoInfo):

  • You can't put any weight on the ankle after an injury
  • There's an obvious deformity or the joint looks out of place
  • You have numbness, tingling, or a cold, pale foot (possible nerve or blood-vessel involvement)
  • After surgery: the area becomes hot, red, increasingly swollen, with fever (possible infection)

When in doubt, it's always reasonable to have an ankle checked rather than wait and hope.

Frequently asked questions

Why does my ankle keep rolling?
The most common reason for an ankle that repeatedly rolls long after a sprain is chronic ankle instability — usually a stretched or torn outer ligament that can no longer hold the joint steady, sometimes combined with weak supporting muscles and balance.

Will chronic ankle instability heal on its own?
A stretched-out ligament generally won't tighten back up by itself. The functional type (weak muscles and balance) often improves a lot with physical therapy, but true mechanical looseness tends to persist without treatment — and repeated sprains can make it worse over time.

Do I need surgery for chronic ankle instability?
Usually not as a first step. Most people start with several months of physical therapy and bracing. Surgery is considered mainly when conservative care fails, the ankle keeps giving way, and imaging confirms structural (mechanical) instability.

How long is recovery after the Broström procedure?
A general guide is about 2 weeks non-weight-bearing, then a walking boot, a brace and physical therapy by around 4 weeks, and a return to sport near 12 weeks — with a minimum of 3–6 months overall. It varies widely by person, and your surgeon sets your timeline.

Is arthroscopic ankle surgery better than open?
Arthroscopic repair tends to mean less trauma and faster early recovery, and it lets the surgeon treat problems inside the joint at the same time. But long-term results are similar to open surgery, and it asks more of the surgeon's skill. Neither is universally "best."

Can repeated ankle sprains damage cartilage?
Yes. Chronic instability can lead to an osteochondral lesion of the talus (OLT) — damage to the cartilage and bone on top of the talus — which is one reason not to ignore an ankle that keeps rolling.

Summary

  • If your ankle keeps rolling or giving way long after a sprain, suspect chronic ankle instability — a stretched outer ligament (often the ATFL) that won't tighten on its own. About 1 in 3 sprains can lead there.
  • Diagnosis is step by step: a hands-on exam, a stress X-ray to see how much the joint opens, and an MRI to check the ligaments and rule out cartilage damage (OLT).
  • Start with physical therapy and bracing. If that fails and the instability is mechanical, the Broström procedure tightens and reinforces the ligament with strong results — but it's not a guarantee, so weigh the benefits and risks with your surgeon.
