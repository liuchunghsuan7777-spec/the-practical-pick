How to Choose a Soundbar (Atmos, Subwoofer & Budget)

Channels, subwoofers, and Dolby Atmos explained — upgrade your TV sound with the right soundbar.

Upgrading from your TV's built-in speakers is one of the easiest ways to make movies, sports, and music sound dramatically better. This guide walks through the specs and trade-offs that actually matter so you can pick a soundbar that fits your room, your habits, and your budget.

Why a soundbar in the first place

Modern flat-panel TVs are thin, and thin cabinets leave little space for speakers that can move air. The result is dialogue that gets lost and low end that simply isn't there. A soundbar packs multiple drivers into a single enclosure that sits under or in front of your screen, giving you clearer voices, more detail, and a wider stage without the clutter of a full component system. For most people it's the biggest audio improvement they can make with the least effort.

What to look for

Start with the channel count, usually written as numbers like 2.0, 2.1, or 5.1. The first number is how many main channels the bar drives, and the second tells you whether a separate subwoofer is included. A 2.0 bar is a straightforward stereo upgrade. A 2.1 setup adds a sub for real bass. A 5.1 system adds side and surround channels for a more enveloping effect, either through extra drivers or included rear speakers.

If you want punch in action scenes and warmth in music, look for a soundbar with subwoofer support. Subs come wired or wireless. Wireless doesn't mean no power cable, it just means the sub talks to the bar over a radio link, so you can tuck it beside a couch or behind a chair without running a signal wire across the floor. Wired subs remove any chance of a dropout but limit placement.

For height and overhead effects, a Dolby Atmos soundbar adds channels that bounce sound off your ceiling or fire it upward, so rain, helicopters, and flyovers seem to come from above rather than straight ahead. Atmos shines in rooms with flat, reasonably low ceilings; vaulted or heavily angled ceilings scatter the reflections and reduce the effect.

Connectivity is where a lot of buyers get tripped up, so check the back panel before you commit:

  • HDMI eARC is the best option. A single cable carries high-quality audio from your TV to the bar and lets you control volume with your TV remote. Make sure your TV has a matching eARC or ARC port.
  • Optical is a reliable fallback that works with almost any TV, though it can't pass the newest high-bandwidth formats.
  • Bluetooth is handy for streaming music from a phone or tablet, and many bars add Wi-Fi for app control and multiroom playback.

Finally, match the bar to your space. As a rough guide, the bar should be about as wide as your TV or a little narrower so it looks balanced and covers the screen's sound field. Confirm the height won't block the bottom edge of the picture or an infrared sensor. Room size matters too: a compact bar can fill a bedroom or small den, while a large open-plan living room benefits from more channels and a capable subwoofer to keep sound from feeling thin.

Budget vs premium

You don't need to spend a fortune to get a meaningful upgrade. A budget soundbar can transform TV audio, sharpen dialogue, and add welcome fullness, which is often all a bedroom, office, or first apartment needs. Entry models typically keep things simple with stereo sound, Bluetooth, and an optical or HDMI input.

Stepping up buys you more of the things that create immersion: a dedicated subwoofer, additional channels, Atmos height effects, HDMI eARC, room-tuning features, and app-based controls. Premium bars also tend to handle loud, complex scenes more cleanly without strain. The honest question is how you'll use it. If you mostly watch news and sitcoms, a simpler bar is money well spent. If you host movie nights, follow the game with friends, or care about music, the extra channels and a strong sub earn their keep.

Setup tips that make a difference

Great gear can still underwhelm if it's placed poorly. Center the bar under the screen and keep its front face unobstructed by media console doors or decor. Give a wireless sub a spot near a wall for a little extra reinforcement, then move it a few feet if the bass sounds boomy or uneven. If your bar includes a calibration or room-correction routine, run it, and take a minute to explore the sound modes, since a dedicated dialogue or night mode can be the feature you reach for most.

Bottom line

Pick the channel layout that matches your room and viewing habits, prioritize an HDMI eARC connection, and add a subwoofer if bass matters to you. A modest bar is a big leap over TV speakers, while more channels, Atmos, and a capable sub reward anyone chasing a cinematic feel. Buy for how you actually watch and listen, and you'll be happy every time you turn it on.
