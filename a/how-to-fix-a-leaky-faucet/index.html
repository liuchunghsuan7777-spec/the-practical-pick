How to Fix a Leaky Faucet: DIY Steps by Faucet Type

Quick answer: To fix a leaky faucet, first shut off the water at the under-sink valves, then identify which of the four faucet types you have (compression, cartridge, ball, or ceramic disc). Most drips are cured by replacing a worn washer, O-ring, or cartridge — a roughly $5–$50 DIY job that takes 30 minutes to a couple of hours. Reassemble, turn the water back on slowly, and test.

Here's the motivation to stop putting it off: a faucet dripping just once per second wastes more than 3,000 gallons of water a year — enough for over 180 showers, according to the EPA. The fix is usually cheaper and easier than people expect. The trick is matching the repair to your faucet type, which is the step most guides skip.

First, Shut Off the Water (Do This Before Anything)

This is the one non-skippable step. Skip it and you'll get a face full of pressurized water the moment you loosen a part.

  • Close the under-sink shut-off valves — there are usually two (hot and cold). Turn them clockwise until they stop.
  • Open the faucet to relieve any remaining pressure and confirm the water is actually off.
  • Plug the drain (or lay a rag over it) so small screws and clips can't disappear down it.
  • Lay a towel in the basin to catch drips and protect the finish.

If your shut-off valves are seized, corroded, or won't fully close, that's your first sign this may be a plumber's job rather than a DIY one. Don't force them.

Identify Your Faucet Type (The Step Everyone Skips)

Before you buy a single part, figure out what you're working with. The repair, the parts, and the cost all depend on the type. Use the table below to narrow it down.

Faucet type How to spot it Usual reason it drips What you'll likely replace
Compression Two separate hot/cold handles you tighten to shut off; oldest design Rubber washer hardens and cracks, so it no longer seals against the valve seat Washer (and O-ring; reseat the valve seat if pitted)
Cartridge Single handle with a smooth turn/lift motion (also some two-handle units) Worn or cracked cartridge, or its internal O-rings and seals degrade The cartridge (sometimes just the O-rings)
Ball Single handle sitting on a rounded cap, common on older kitchen sinks Worn springs and rubber seats inside the valve body A ball-faucet repair kit (springs, seats, sometimes the cam/ball)
Ceramic disc Single handle on a wide, cylindrical body; modern, premium feel Rarely drips — usually sediment between the discs or a failed seal Clean the discs/seals, or replace the disc cartridge

If you're stuck between two, the fastest tell is the handle count and motion: two handles you screw down = compression; one smooth handle = cartridge, ball, or disc, which you then tell apart by the body shape and what's under the cap.

Tools & Parts You'll Need

You don't need a plumber's full kit. Most faucet repairs use basic hand tools plus one correct replacement part.

Tools
- Adjustable wrench and slip-joint pliers
- Flathead and Phillips screwdrivers
- A set of hex (Allen) keys — many handle set screws are hex
- A basin wrench for tight nuts up under the sink (optional but a back-saver)
- A rag (to protect finishes and grip stuck parts)

Parts (buy to match your type)
- A washer / O-ring assortment for compression faucets
- The correct replacement cartridge for cartridge faucets
- A ball-faucet repair kit (springs + seats) for ball faucets
- Plumber's tape and plumber's grease (see the callout — they are not the same thing)

Plumber's tape vs. plumber's grease — don't mix these up.
Plumber's tape (PTFE / Teflon tape) seals threaded connections — you wrap it around threads before screwing parts together. Plumber's grease (a silicone lubricant) goes on O-rings, cartridges, and moving parts so they slide and seal without tearing. Using one where the other belongs is a classic cause of a repair that still leaks. Keep both on hand and use each for its job.

Pro tip on parts: cartridges and washers are often brand- and model-specific (Moen, Delta, and Kohler all differ). The reliable move is to take the old part to the hardware store and match it, or read the model number etched on the cartridge — rather than guessing that one "universal" part fits.

Step-by-Step Repairs by Faucet Type

Water off? Drain plugged? Good. Find your type below.

Cartridge faucet (the most common DIY fix)

  1. Pop off the decorative cap on top of the handle (a flat screwdriver wrapped in tape usually pries it gently).
  2. Remove the set screw underneath — often a small hex/Allen screw — and lift the handle straight off.
  3. Remove the retaining nut or clip holding the cartridge, then pull the cartridge straight up. If it's stuck, grip it with pliers padded by a rag. Note the orientation of the notches before it comes out — the new one has to go in the same way.
  4. Match the replacement to the old cartridge (brand, model number, or take it to the store).
  5. Install the new cartridge with a thin film of plumber's grease, aligning the notches exactly as the old one sat.
  6. Reassemble in reverse: retaining nut, handle, set screw, cap.

Compression faucet (two handles)

  1. Pry off the handle cap, remove the handle screw, and lift the handle.
  2. Unscrew the packing/retaining nut and back out the stem.
  3. At the bottom of the stem, replace the worn rubber washer (held by a small brass screw) and swap the O-ring while you're in there.
  4. Inspect the valve seat down in the faucet body. If it feels rough or pitted, it won't seal against a new washer — you'll need to reface or replace the seat (a seat wrench helps), or call a pro if it's badly corroded.
  5. Reassemble and repeat for the other handle if it leaks too.

Ball faucet (rounded cap, often kitchen)

  1. Loosen the handle set screw and remove the handle, then the cap/collar.
  2. Use the repair kit to replace the rubber seats and springs down in the valve body — these are the usual culprits.
  3. If the ball itself is corroded, replace it too (kits often include one). Reassemble carefully; ball faucets have several small parts that must seat in order.

Ceramic disc faucet

  1. Remove the handle and the cap to expose the disc cartridge.
  2. Ceramic disc faucets rarely fail — most "leaks" here are mineral sediment. Gently clean the discs and seals and reseat them.
  3. If cleaning doesn't stop the drip, replace the disc cartridge with the matching part.

Reassemble & Test

Put everything back in reverse order, snug but not over-tightened (over-tightening cracks plastic parts and crushes seals). Then:

  • Turn the water back on slowly at the shut-off valves — a fast surge can pop a freshly seated part.
  • Run the faucet, then check for drips at the spout (usually a washer/cartridge issue) and at the base (usually an O-ring).
  • Let it sit a few minutes and look again. A slow leak can take a moment to reappear.

Still Dripping? Quick Troubleshooting

  • Drip from the spout → washer, cartridge, or seats not sealing. Recheck the part and its orientation; confirm you bought the right model.
  • Leak from the base of the spout → almost always a worn O-ring; replace it.
  • Cartridge installed but still drips → it may be the wrong cartridge, seated backward, or the body/valve seat is damaged.
  • Pitted or corroded valve seat that a new washer can't seal → reface, replace the seat, or call a plumber.
  • Leak you can't isolate, or it's behind/under the wall → stop and call a pro.

DIY vs. Hiring a Plumber: Cost

Here's the honest cost picture so you can decide. Treat these as typical ranges — they vary by region, faucet, and date.

DIY Hire a plumber
Parts ~$5–$50 (a washer/O-ring is a few dollars; a cartridge more) Included in the bill
Tools ~$20–$50 if you need to buy basics (one-time) None
Labor Your time Most sources put the national average around ~$275; labor commonly $45–$200/hour, ~1 hour for a simple job
Time 30 min to a couple of hours your first time A scheduled visit

For a standard sink faucet with a washer, O-ring, or cartridge swap, DIY usually wins on cost and is well within reach for a first-timer.

When to call a plumber instead. Hand it to a pro if the leak is behind or inside a wall, in a bathtub or shower valve, at an outdoor/hose bib, if you find a corroded valve seat or seized parts you can't free, or if a shut-off valve won't close. Don't force seized parts — you can crack the faucet body or burst a supply line, turning a cheap repair into an expensive flood.

FAQ

Why is my faucet still dripping after I turned it off?
Because the internal seal is worn — the handle being "off" no longer presses a good washer, O-ring, or cartridge tight enough to stop the flow. Replacing that worn part is the fix.

How do I know what type of faucet I have?
Count the handles and feel the motion. Two handles you screw down = compression. One smooth handle = cartridge, ball, or ceramic disc, told apart by the body shape and what's under the cap (see the table above).

Do I need to turn off the water to fix a faucet?
Yes. Always close the under-sink shut-off valves first and relieve the pressure by opening the faucet. Skipping this sprays pressurized water everywhere.

How do I know which replacement cartridge to buy?
Match the brand and model — many cartridges have a number etched on them. The safest method is to remove the old one and take it to the store rather than assuming a universal part fits.

Is it cheaper to fix a faucet myself?
Usually, yes. DIY parts run roughly $5–$50, while hiring a plumber averages around $275 in many cost surveys. Simple washer/O-ring/cartridge swaps are the most DIY-friendly.

Can I fix a leaky faucet without replacing parts?
Sometimes — a ceramic disc faucet often just needs the discs and seals cleaned of mineral sediment. But a hardened washer or cracked cartridge has to be replaced; cleaning won't restore a worn seal.

Final Takeaway

A dripping faucet feels intimidating until you break it into two moves: shut off the water, then match the repair to your faucet type. Cartridge faucets get a new cartridge, compression faucets get a washer and O-ring, ball faucets get a repair kit, and ceramic disc faucets usually just need a cleaning. Take the old part to the store to match it, keep plumber's tape and grease straight, and turn the water back on slowly. For most kitchen and bathroom sinks, that turns a wasteful drip into a 30-minute, low-cost Saturday fix — and you only call a plumber when the leak is behind a wall, in a tub/shower valve, or fighting a seized part.
