How to Choose a Cordless Drill (Voltage, Chuck & Kits)

Voltage, chuck size, battery, and drill vs driver — a beginner-friendly guide to picking a cordless drill.

A cordless drill is one of the most useful tools you can own, whether you're hanging shelves, assembling furniture, or tackling a weekend remodel. This guide walks through the specs that actually matter so you can match a tool to the work you do most.

What to look for

The specs on the box can feel like alphabet soup, but a few of them drive most of the difference in how a drill performs.

Voltage. Voltage is a rough proxy for power. A 12V tool is lighter and easier to handle for one-handed work, cabinet installs, and light assembly. An 18V or 20V tool (these two labels describe nearly the same battery, just measured differently) delivers more torque for driving long fasteners, boring larger holes, and working in denser material. For most homeowners, an 18/20V platform is the more versatile choice, while a 12V shines when comfort and reach matter more than raw muscle.

Chuck size. The chuck is the adjustable jaw that grips the bit. A 3/8-inch chuck covers the majority of household drilling and driving. A 1/2-inch chuck accepts larger-diameter bits and is the better pick if you expect to use spade bits, hole saws, or heavier accessories. Keyless chucks let you swap bits by hand, which is the standard on modern tools.

Brushless motors. A brushless motor runs cooler and more efficiently than an older brushed design, which generally translates to longer runtime per charge, less maintenance, and a lighter tool for a given amount of power. Brushless has become common, and it's worth prioritizing if it fits your budget.

Battery platform and amp-hours. The battery may be the most important long-term decision, because once you buy into a platform, your future tools and batteries usually need to match it. Batteries are rated in amp-hours (Ah); a higher Ah rating means more runtime between charges but also more weight. A smaller battery keeps the tool nimble for quick jobs, while a larger one suits longer sessions. Buying tools that share one battery system saves money and clutter over time.

Clutch settings. The clutch is the numbered collar behind the chuck. It lets the drill slip once a fastener reaches a set resistance, which helps you avoid stripping screw heads or sinking fasteners too deep. More clutch settings give you finer control across different materials and screw sizes.

Weight and ergonomics. Weight matters more than people expect, especially for overhead work or long stretches. A compact cordless drill is easier on your wrist and fits into tight cabinets and corners, while a full-size tool trades some of that comfort for added capability. If you can, hold the tool and check how the grip and balance feel in your hand.

Drill/driver vs. impact driver vs. combo kit

A standard drill/driver is the all-rounder: it drills holes and drives screws, and its adjustable clutch gives you control over delicate fasteners. An impact driver is a specialist. It uses rotational bursts to sink long screws and lag bolts with far less effort and wrist strain, but it isn't ideal for precise holes or fine work.

Many shoppers end up wanting both, which is why a drill/driver combo kit is such a popular starting point. A kit typically pairs the two tools on one battery platform, often with a couple of batteries and a charger, so you get complementary tools that share power and a carrying case. If you're building a toolset from scratch, a combo kit is usually more economical than buying each piece separately, and it covers a wider range of tasks right out of the box.

Matching the tool to your projects

Think about the work you actually do. For light, occasional tasks around the house, a compact 12V drill/driver is comfortable and more than capable. For general DIY and home improvement, an 18/20V brushless tool hits a strong balance of power and runtime. If your projects lean toward decks, framing, or repetitive fastening, adding an impact driver, or starting with a combo kit, will make the work noticeably easier.

Also weigh the accessories you'll need. A basic bit set, a few driver bits, and a spare battery go a long way, and factoring those into your plan helps you avoid a second trip. As a general safety habit, wear eye protection, keep the tool's manual handy, and let the drill do the work rather than forcing it.

Bottom line

Start with the battery platform and voltage that fit your typical projects, then look for a brushless motor, an appropriate chuck size, and a comfortable weight. A capable cordless drill handles most household needs on its own, a compact model wins on comfort and reach, and a drill/driver combo kit is the most flexible way to cover both drilling and heavy driving from day one.
