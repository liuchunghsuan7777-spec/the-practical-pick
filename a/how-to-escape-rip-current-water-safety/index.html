How to Escape a Rip Current + Summer Water Safety Tips

Caught in a rip current? Don't fight it. Stay calm, float on your back, and swim parallel to the shore (along the beach) until you're out of the pull—then angle back in. If you can't swim out, float, face the beach, wave one arm, and yell for help.

This guide covers the rest of summer water safety, too: what to do before you get in the water, how to keep kids safe, the hidden dangers of lakes and rivers, why cold water can drop even strong swimmers, and what to do if you see someone drowning.

One thing up front: this is general safety information, not professional rescue or medical training. In a real emergency, call 911 (or your local emergency number) and follow lifeguards and the dispatcher. The best preparation is a hands-on class from the American Red Cross or the American Heart Association.

How to escape a rip current (don't fight it)

A rip current is a strong, narrow channel of water that flows away from the beach, back out to sea. It can pull even a strong swimmer offshore surprisingly fast, and rip currents are the cause of most beach rescues. (Source: NOAA/NWS; USLA)

The instinct to swim straight back to the beach is exactly what gets people in trouble. The current moves faster than you can swim against it, so fighting it head-on just burns the energy you need to survive. (Source: NOAA NESDIS/NWS)

Here's what to do instead:

  1. Stay calm. Panic wastes energy and air.
  2. Float. Flip onto your back and float to get your breathing under control.
  3. Swim parallel to the shore. Swim sideways, along the beach, to get out of the narrow band of current. Rip currents are usually narrow and tend to weaken just past the line of breaking waves.
  4. Then angle back in. Once you're out of the pull, swim back to shore at an angle, riding the waves in.

If you can't escape, don't keep fighting. Float or tread water, face the shore, and wave one arm and yell for help. Lifeguards and others on the beach are watching for exactly that signal. A simple way to remember it: "Flip, Float, and Follow"—flip onto your back, float to stay calm, and follow the safest path out.

Check the forecast before you go. The National Weather Service publishes a Beach/Surf Zone Forecast with a daily rip current risk rating (low, moderate, or high) for many coastal areas at weather.gov. A quick look before you leave tells you what conditions to expect. And whenever you can, swim at a lifeguarded beach and stay between the flags—lifeguards mark the safest area for the day's conditions. (Source: NWS; USLA; American Red Cross)

Water safety tips before you get in

Most water trouble starts in the first moments, or in the moments people let their guard down. A few habits cut the risk dramatically. (Source: American Red Cross; NDPA; CDC)

  • Swim where there's a lifeguard, and stay between the flags. Lifeguards mark the safest zone for current conditions and can respond fast.
  • Never swim alone—use the buddy system. Have someone watching from shore, and keep an eye on each other in the water.
  • No alcohol or drugs before or while swimming—or while you're the one supervising swimmers. Alcohol impairs balance, coordination, and your judgment of danger.
  • Wear a life jacket that fits. Choose a U.S. Coast Guard–approved life jacket and check the label for the right weight and size. It's essential in open water and on boats, and a real help for weaker swimmers.
  • Check the weather and know your limits. Avoid storms and high winds, and don't push past the distance or depth you can comfortably handle.
  • Learn to swim. Formal swim lessons can significantly lower the risk of drowning for both kids and adults. (Source: CDC)

A note on cold water: ease in gradually rather than jumping straight into cold open water. There's a real physiological reason for this, covered in the cold water shock section below.

Keeping kids safe in the water

This is the part to take most seriously. Drowning is the leading cause of death for children ages 1–4, and a top cause for ages 5–14. About 4,000 people die from unintentional drowning in the U.S. each year—roughly 11 a day—and a large share of those are children and teens. (Source: CDC; verify the latest CDC figures, updated 2024)

The hardest myth to unlearn: drowning is fast and silent. It rarely looks like the loud, splashing struggle people picture. A child can slip under quietly in seconds. (Source: CDC; American Red Cross)

  • Watch closely and without distractions. For young kids and weak swimmers, that means "touch supervision"—staying within arm's reach. Put the phone away. Don't assume someone else is watching.
  • Use life jackets, not pool toys. Water wings, inner tubes, and floaties are not safety devices. They can deflate, flip, or slip off. For young or weak swimmers, use a U.S. Coast Guard–approved life jacket instead. (Source: American Red Cross; Water Safety USA)
  • Teach kids to never chase a drifting toy. If a float, ball, or toy drifts away, the rule is: let it go and ask an adult—don't go after it.
  • No rough play in the water. Pushing, dunking, and grabbing can turn into drowning in an instant.
  • Fence the pool. For backyard pools, a four-sided fence keeps young children from reaching the water unsupervised. (Source: CDC)

Lake and river (swimming hole) safety

Natural water hides dangers a pool doesn't. The surface can look calm while the water below holds surprises. (Source: American Red Cross)

  • You can't see the hazards. Murky water hides sudden drop-offs, underwater rocks, branches, debris, and currents. Dams create powerful, deadly undertows.
  • Even slow- or shallow-looking water can move you. A current you can't see can pull you under or sweep you downstream. (Source: Texas Parks & Wildlife; American Red Cross)
  • Stay out after heavy rain. Swollen rivers and fast-moving water can stay dangerous for days. A good rule: stay out of rivers and swimming holes for at least 48 hours after heavy rain or flooding. Rain upstream can raise the water where you are, even if it's sunny over your head. (Source: American Red Cross; flood-water recreation guidance)
  • Never dive into unknown water. Dive only where it's clearly marked safe—generally at least about 9 feet deep with no underwater obstacles. Diving into shallow or murky water is a leading cause of serious spinal injuries. (Source: American Red Cross)
  • Mind your footing. Lake and river bottoms can be slick with algae and lined with sharp rocks. Water shoes can help with traction and protect your feet, though they don't make a risky spot safe.

Cold water shock: the danger strong swimmers underestimate

Here's a risk many confident swimmers have never heard of. Sudden immersion in cold water—roughly below 59°F (15°C)—triggers an involuntary gasp followed by rapid, uncontrolled breathing. This is called cold water shock, and it can cause sudden drowning even for excellent swimmers, often within the first minute. (Source: NWS; Cold Water Safety)

It can also spike your heart rate and blood pressure and, in some cases, trigger dangerous heart rhythms. (Source: NWS)

This matters most in lakes and quarries, which are often much colder below the surface than the warm air suggests—even in midsummer. So:

  • Ease in gradually rather than plunging into cold open water.
  • If it happens, don't panic. Float on your back and let the gasping pass. Once your breathing is back under control, then start moving toward safety. (Source: NWS; cold-water guidance)

If you see someone drowning: reach or throw, don't go

This single rule saves lives—including yours. The most important thing to know is don't jump in. Untrained rescuers who enter the water often become drowning victims themselves, which is a major reason single drownings turn into multiple drownings. (Source: American Red Cross, "Reach or Throw, Don't Go")

Instead:

  1. Call 911 first (or send someone to call) and alert the lifeguard if there is one.
  2. Reach. From a stable position on land or a dock, extend a pole, oar, paddle, towel, branch, or even your arm.
  3. Throw. Toss anything that floats—a life ring, rope, cooler, or empty jug—to the person so they can grab on.

Most rescues are actually made by bystanders who happen to be close, not by professionals. That's exactly why the safe method—reach or throw, never go—matters so much. (Source: American Red Cross)

Hands-Only CPR and AEDs (the general idea)

If someone is pulled from the water and is unresponsive and not breathing, CPR can save their life—and immediate CPR can double or triple the chance of survival from cardiac arrest. (Source: American Heart Association)

At a concept level, Hands-Only CPR is two steps: (1) call 911, and (2) push hard and fast in the center of the chest, about 100–120 compressions per minute, until help arrives. (Source: AHA)

An AED (automated external defibrillator) is designed for untrained bystanders. Turn it on and follow the spoken prompts—it analyzes the heart rhythm and tells you whether and when to deliver a shock. (Source: AHA; American Red Cross)

One important note for drowning specifically: because drowning cardiac arrest usually comes from a lack of oxygen, rescue breaths matter more than in a typical adult collapse. Teaching that safely is beyond what an article can do. So keep it simple in the moment: call 911, start CPR if you're trained, use an AED if one is available, and follow the dispatcher's instructions. The real preparation is a hands-on CPR/AED class from the Red Cross or AHA—well worth a few hours before summer.

After a near-drowning: watch for delayed symptoms

Someone who was pulled from the water and seems fine can still develop breathing problems later. If a person was submerged or struggled in the water and then has trouble breathing, a persistent cough, chest pain, extreme tiredness, or confusion, they should be checked by a medical professional—even if they look okay at first. When in doubt, call 911. (Source: general medical / American Red Cross guidance)

You may hear sensational terms like "dry drowning" or "secondary drowning." Doctors discourage those labels. The practical takeaway is simpler: after any significant near-drowning, get a medical evaluation rather than waiting to see what happens.

Frequently asked questions

What do I do if I'm caught in a rip current?
Don't try to swim straight back to the beach against the current—you'll just exhaust yourself. Stay calm, float on your back, and swim parallel to the shore until you're out of the pull, then angle back in. If you can't get out, float, face the beach, wave one arm, and yell for help. (Source: NOAA/NWS; USLA)

Are floaties and water wings safe for my child?
No—they're toys, not safety devices. They can deflate, flip, or slip off in an instant. For young or weak swimmers, use a U.S. Coast Guard–approved life jacket that fits, and keep watching your child closely the whole time. (Source: American Red Cross; Water Safety USA)

Is it safe to swim in a river after it rains?
Be very cautious. Heavy rain can make rivers and swimming holes fast and dangerous for days, even when the surface looks calm and the sun is out. A good rule is to stay out for at least 48 hours after heavy rain or flooding. (Source: American Red Cross)

Why is cold water dangerous if I'm a strong swimmer?
Sudden cold-water immersion (below about 59°F/15°C) triggers an involuntary gasp and rapid breathing—cold water shock—that can cause drowning within the first minute, regardless of how well you swim. Ease in gradually, and if it happens, float on your back until your breathing settles. (Source: NWS; Cold Water Safety)

Should I jump in to save someone who's drowning?
Generally, no. Untrained rescuers who jump in often drown too. Call 911 first, then reach with a pole, towel, or branch, or throw something that floats. Reach or throw—don't go. (Source: American Red Cross)

What if someone seems fine after almost drowning?
Keep watching them. If they develop trouble breathing, a persistent cough, chest pain, severe fatigue, or confusion, get medical care—call 911 if it's serious. After any significant near-drowning, a medical evaluation is the safe choice even if they look okay.
