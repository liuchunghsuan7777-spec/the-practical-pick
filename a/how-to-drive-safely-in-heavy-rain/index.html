How to Drive Safely in Heavy Rain: 8 Essential Tips

Driving safely in heavy rain comes down to three things: slow down and leave extra space, keep your view clear (wipers, defogger, low-beam headlights), and never drive into a flooded road. Get those right and you've handled most of the risk.

If gripping the wheel in a downpour makes you tense, you're not overreacting. Nearly half of all weather-related crashes happen during rainfall (NHTSA, via AAA Exchange — Wet Weather Driving Tips). This guide walks you through eight practical habits, from preventing and recovering a skid to clearing a fogged-up windshield and knowing exactly what to do if the road ahead is underwater. Quick summary: (1) Ease off the gas and stay well under the limit. (2) Keep your windshield, wipers, and headlights working for you. (3) If a road is flooded, turn around and find another way.

1. Slow down and leave extra space

The first thing to change in the rain is your speed. Wet roads lengthen your stopping distance, so braking that feels normal on a dry day can come up short on a wet one. The simple fix: ease off and drive well below the posted limit, and avoid hard braking or sharp turns (AAA Exchange — Wet Weather Driving Tips).


On wet roads, slow well under the limit and extend your following distance — stopping distance is longer than on dry pavement.

  • Following distance: On a dry road, the usual advice is to stay at least a few seconds behind the car ahead. In the rain, increase that gap — give yourself noticeably more room so you have time to react if traffic stops suddenly.
  • Headlights on, low beam: Turn your headlights on even during daytime rain. It helps you see, but mostly it helps other drivers see you. Keep them on low beam — high beams reflect off the rain and worsen glare (Les Schwab — Driving in Rain). Many states actually require headlights whenever your wipers are running.
  • Signal early: When you change lanes, turn your blinker on a little sooner than usual. Everyone's reaction time is slower in the rain, including the driver behind you.
  • Skip cruise control: Don't use cruise control in the rain. If you start to hydroplane with it on, the system can misread what's happening and even add throttle, and you lose a step in regaining control (Les Schwab; ePermitTest — Hydroplaning).

Before storm season, a full pre-drive check — tire pressure, coolant, brakes — is worth doing too. We cover that in detail in a separate guide.

2. What is hydroplaning — and how to recover

Hydroplaning is when a layer of water gets between your tires and the road, so the tires ride on top of the water and lose grip. When that happens, your steering and brakes briefly stop responding — which is exactly why it's so unnerving (Wawanesa — Hydroplaning).


Hydroplaning happens when water lifts the tires off the road. To recover, ease off the gas and steer straight — never brake hard or jerk the wheel.

Here's the part most people don't realize: it takes surprisingly little water. As little as 1/10 of an inch (0.1") of standing water can cause it (Les Schwab). And the risk climbs sharply once you're going above about 35 mph — though that's a "risk rises significantly," not a hard on/off threshold; it also depends on water depth, tire wear, and your vehicle's weight (Les Schwab, citing NHTSA data).

How to avoid it:

  1. Slow down, especially through standing water or puddles you can see ahead.
  2. Keep good tread on your tires (the penny test in Tip 4 takes ten seconds).
  3. Keep tires properly inflated so they can channel water away.
  4. Skip cruise control in the rain.

If your car does start to hydroplane, the instinct to slam the brakes or yank the wheel is the worst thing you can do. Instead: ease your foot off the gas, hold the wheel steady, and steer gently toward where you want to go until you feel grip return. No hard braking, no sudden inputs (Wawanesa; AAA Exchange).

3. How to defog your windshield fast

A surprisingly dangerous part of rainy driving is a fogged-up windshield — blurred glass is a crash risk all by itself. It fogs because moisture in the cabin air condenses on the glass when there's a temperature and humidity difference between inside and outside, and rainy days push interior humidity up (Erie Insurance; Consumer Reports).


Defog fast: A/C on, defroster aimed at the glass, and recirculation off so the car pulls in drier outside air.

The fastest way to clear it:

  • Hit the DEFROST (defogger) button — the setting with the windshield-and-wavy-lines icon. It automatically engages the A/C and a high fan, blowing dry air right at the glass (Consumer Reports).
  • Turn the A/C on. Air conditioning dehumidifies the cabin air, which is what actually clears the fog — even on a warm day.
  • Turn OFF recirculation. This is the key step. With recirculation on, the same humid cabin air keeps cycling and the fog comes right back. Switch to fresh/outside air so the car pulls in drier air from outside (Consumer Reports; Erie).
  • Crack the windows a bit if you can — it dumps humid cabin air even faster.

One thing not to do: don't wipe the glass with your hand or a cloth. It leaves streaks, the fog returns, and your visibility ends up worse (Erie Insurance).

A musty A/C smell is a different problem with a different fix (it's usually the cabin air filter), so don't confuse it with defogging. We cover that in a separate article.

4. Check your wipers and tires before storm season

Rainy-weather safety really comes down to two things: seeing clearly (wipers) and gripping the road (tires). Checking just these two before the rainy season starts buys a lot of peace of mind.

Wipers — streaks and chatter mean replace

  • Replacement interval: Plan on new wiper blades every 6 to 12 months (AAA). Intense sun, heavy rain, and freezing winters all break down the rubber faster, so check them seasonally — a good cue is when you change your clocks.
  • Replace now, regardless of age, if you see streaking or smearing, missed or skipped areas, hear chattering or squeaking, or notice cracked rubber or a bent frame (AAA; AutoZone — Signs You Need New Windshield Wipers).

Tires — worn tread means slipping

Wet-road slipping and hydroplaning are tied directly to tire tread. The self-check is quick:

  • The penny test: Insert a penny into a tread groove with Lincoln's head upside down and facing you. If you can see all of Lincoln's head, your tread is at or below 2/32" — time for new tires. If part of his head is covered, you still have usable tread (Bridgestone; Discount Tire).
  • Legal minimum, 2/32": The U.S. DOT recommends replacing tires at 2/32", and many states legally require it. Tires also have built-in wear-indicator bars in the grooves at that depth (Discount Tire).
  • For wet safety, replace earlier — around 4/32": Below roughly 4/32", wet stopping distance grows and hydroplaning risk rises sharply, so many tire pros suggest not waiting all the way down to the legal limit (Discount Tire; Les Schwab).
  • Keep tires properly inflated. Correct pressure helps wet grip and water channeling — don't deliberately let air out. (Detailed PSI and TPMS guidance lives in our summer car-check guide.)

5. ⚠️ Flooded roads — Turn Around, Don't Drown (the most important rule)

This is the most important section in the whole guide, because it's a life-safety issue. The rule is simple and it has a name: Turn Around, Don't Drown (National Weather Service; FEMA).


Never drive into a flooded road — just 12 inches of water floats most cars. If your car is sinking, follow AAA's S-U-R-E steps: Stay calm, Unbuckle, Roll down or break a window, Exit.

  • Never drive in — turn around. Don't drive through a flooded road, underpass, or around barriers. You can't tell how deep the water is or whether the road underneath has been washed out (NWS; FEMA).
  • It takes shockingly little water. Just 6 inches of moving water can knock an adult off their feet and stall many cars; 12 inches of rushing water can carry away most vehicles; and 2 feet can sweep away SUVs and pickup trucks (Ready.gov; NWS). Over half of all flood-related drownings involve a vehicle driven into floodwater (NWS/FEMA).
  • If water is rising around your car, get out and move to higher ground if you can do so safely — both you and the car can be swept away fast (Ready.gov).
  • If your engine stalls in water, don't try to restart it. Get out instead. (Restarting a flooded engine can cause serious damage — confirm and cite a US auto source such as Kelley Blue Book before publishing. NEEDS SOURCE.)

How to escape a sinking car — the AAA S-U-R-E method

If your car ends up in the water, AAA's escape plan spells out S-U-R-E (AAA — How to Escape a Sinking Vehicle):

  • S — Stay calm.
  • U — Unbuckle your seatbelt and help passengers do the same.
  • R — Roll down or break a window immediately. Power windows usually keep working for up to about a minute after hitting water; once water reaches the window, pressure can keep it from rolling down. If needed, use an escape tool on a side window.
  • E — Exit quickly and swim to safety.

Two important caveats:

  • Escape tools break tempered side glass but cannot break laminated glass, which is increasingly common on newer cars. AAA recommends a spring-loaded escape tool over a hammer-style one (AAA).
  • If you can't break the window, move to an air pocket toward the back of the car and wait until the cabin nearly fills with water — once the pressure inside and outside equalizes, a door can usually be opened (AAA). This is a last resort; the safer message is always to get out before the car submerges.

A note on electric vehicles: if an EV is flooded, the safest move is to follow your automaker's specific instructions. Claims about immediate shock risk aren't well established, so don't rely on assumptions — defer to the manufacturer's guidance.

6. Tips for night rain and deep puddles

  • When you can't see lane lines: At night, wet pavement reflects light and lane markings can disappear. Slow down and follow the center line or the path of the car ahead as a reference while you watch the road carefully.
  • Puddles and standing water: If you don't know how deep it is, don't drive in. If you absolutely must pass through, slow down well beforehand.
  • If you have to stop: When visibility gets too bad to continue, pull over somewhere safe and turn on your hazard lights so other drivers can see you.

A hot, stuffy cabin can make fogging worse, too, because of how you end up opening and closing windows. We cover keeping your car cool in a separate guide.

Frequently asked questions

How fast can you hydroplane?
There's no single magic number, but hydroplaning risk rises sharply above about 35 mph. It also depends on how much water is on the road, how worn your tires are, and how heavy your vehicle is — so slowing down is your best defense (Les Schwab, citing NHTSA data).

What should I do if my car starts to hydroplane?
Don't brake hard and don't jerk the wheel. Ease your foot off the gas, hold the wheel steady, and steer gently in the direction you want to go until grip returns (Wawanesa; AAA).

Why do my windows fog up when it rains?
Humid cabin air condenses on the cooler glass. The fastest fix is the DEFROST setting with the A/C on and recirculation off, so the car blows dry outside air at the windshield (Consumer Reports; Erie Insurance).

How much water does it take to float a car?
About 12 inches of rushing water can carry away most cars, and just 6 inches can stall many vehicles. Never drive into a flooded road (Ready.gov; NWS).

When should I replace my tires for wet driving?
The legal minimum is 2/32" (the penny test), but for safer wet traction many pros recommend replacing earlier, around 4/32" (Discount Tire; Les Schwab).

Wrap-up: three habits for safer rainy driving

Driving in heavy rain really does come down to three things:

  • Slow down and leave extra room — ease off the gas, stay well under the limit, and keep cruise control off.
  • Keep your view clear — low-beam headlights, working wipers, and the defroster with A/C on and recirculation off.
  • Never drive into a flooded road — turn around, and if your car is sinking, remember S-U-R-E.

Check your wipers and tires once before the rainy season and you'll feel a lot calmer the next time the sky opens up. Drive safe.
