How to Choose a Space Heater: Types, Wattage & Safety

Quick answer: To choose a space heater, match three things to your room. Pick the type (ceramic for fast spot heat, oil-filled for steady whole-room warmth, infrared for drafty rooms or spot-warming a person), size the wattage to the space (about 10 watts per square foot — a 1,500W heater suits roughly a 150 sq ft room), and insist on the safety features (UL/ETL/CSA certification, a tip-over switch, overheat shutoff, and cool-touch housing). One rule overrides everything: plug it straight into a wall outlet — never an extension cord or power strip — and keep 3 feet of clearance on every side.

This guide walks you through each of those three decisions so you can pick the right heater for your room the first time, without overpaying or buying something unsafe.

First, the Safety Reality (Why This Matters)

Space heaters are a small slice of home heating equipment but cause an outsized share of the harm. According to the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA), portable and stationary space heaters accounted for roughly one-third of home heating fires in 2016–2020 — and the large majority of the deaths and injuries from heating-equipment fires. An earlier NFPA window found portable heaters were only about 3% of heating fires but involved 41% of the fatal ones. Home heating fires peak in December, January, and February.

The point isn't to scare you off a space heater — millions are used safely every winter. The point is that the safety features and where you place the heater matter just as much as how much heat it puts out. That's why this guide treats certification, a tip-over switch, and the 3-foot rule as non-negotiable, not optional extras. Keep that in mind as you compare types.

Space Heater Types Compared

There are four common types, and they heat in genuinely different ways. The right one depends on whether you want fast spot heat or slow, steady warmth — and how the room is laid out.

Ceramic (fan-forced)

The most common and usually the cheapest type. An internal ceramic element heats up, and a fan blows that warmth into the room. Ceramics heat fast, are small and portable, and are great for quickly taking the chill off a room or warming a single spot like a desk. The trade-offs: they stop warming almost immediately once they shut off, and the fan makes some noise.

Oil-filled radiator

Sealed metal fins filled with oil (the oil never needs refilling) warm up and radiate heat slowly, steadily, and quietly. They're slow to get going, but they hold warmth even after cycling off, which makes them the best pick for sustained, whole-room heating — a room you sit in for hours, or a bedroom. The downsides: they're heavy, slow to start, and the surface gets hot.

Infrared / radiant

Infrared heaters warm objects and people directly, like sunlight, rather than heating the air. That makes them efficient for drafty or poorly insulated spaces and for spot-warming a person — say, at a garage workbench. The catch: the glowing element runs hot and carries a higher burn or ignition risk if something is too close, so they need extra clearance.

Micathermic

Mica-panel heaters deliver both convective and radiant heat from a slim, often wall-mountable profile, and warm a room fairly quickly. They're less common, and the panels get quite hot to the touch.

A handy way reviewers frame it: a ceramic heater is the sprinter (instant, fan-driven, perfect for a quick blast), and an oil-filled radiator is the marathoner (gradual, holds heat, quiet for the long haul). That analogy isn't an official standard, but it captures the real difference well.

How Many Watts Do You Need? (Sizing)

This is the number everyone searches for, and the rule of thumb is simple: about 10 watts per square foot. So a standard 1,500-watt heater is sized for roughly a 150 sq ft room — a typical bedroom or home office. Most electric space heaters land between 400 and 1,500 watts, and "personal" heaters under about 900 watts often underperform if you're trying to heat a whole room rather than just yourself.

Why does almost every full-size heater top out at exactly 1,500 watts? Because that's the practical ceiling for a standard US 120-volt, 15-amp household circuit. A 1,500W heater draws about 12.5 amps, and a 15-amp circuit maxes out around 1,800 watts — so 1,500W leaves a safety margin. It also explains why you can't just "buy a bigger one" to heat a large room on one outlet.

Table B — Wattage by room size (≈10W/sq ft rule of thumb)

Room size Approx. watts Typical heater
Personal / ~75 sq ft ~750 W Personal/desk ceramic
Small room / ~150 sq ft ~1,500 W Standard 1,500W heater
Larger or poorly insulated 1,500W + supplement, or better insulation Oil-filled, or heat one zone at a time

A caveat worth flagging: the 10W/sq ft figure assumes roughly 8-foot ceilings and average insulation. Cold climates, high ceilings, big windows, or drafty rooms all need more — and since 1,500W is the circuit ceiling, the realistic move for a big or leaky room is to heat one zone you're actually in rather than expecting one heater to warm a whole open floor plan.

Safety Features Checklist — What Must Be on the Box

Before you compare looks or price, check for these. The first three are the difference between a safe heater and a fire risk.

Table C — Safety-feature buy checklist

Feature What it does Must-have?
UL / ETL / CSA mark Independently safety-tested by a recognized lab Yes — don't buy without it
Tip-over switch Shuts the heater off if it's knocked over (~10 sec) Yes
Overheat protection Shuts it off if it gets too hot (~2 min) Yes
Cool-touch housing Lowers burn risk Yes if you have kids or pets
Thermostat / timer Cycles off at target temp; auto-off Strongly recommended

A few notes on the list:

  • Certification mark. Look for a UL, ETL (Intertek), or CSA mark — these are Nationally Recognized Testing Laboratories. An uncertified heater is the single biggest red flag; skip it no matter how cheap.
  • Tip-over switch and overheat protection should both be standard on any quality heater. The ~10-second and ~2-minute timings are typical guidance, not a guaranteed spec, but the features themselves are essential.
  • A thermostat isn't just convenience — it cycles the heater off when the room hits temperature, which is both safer and cheaper to run than a heater that blasts full-power nonstop.

Safe-Use Rules (Placement & Power)

The best heater is only as safe as how you use it. These rules come straight from NFPA, ESFI, and the U.S. Fire Administration:

  • Keep 3 feet of clearance. Nothing that can burn — bedding, curtains, furniture, paper — within 3 feet, and treat that as a kid- and pet-free zone too.
  • Plug directly into a wall outlet. Never an extension cord or power strip. A 1,500W heater's ~12.5-amp draw can overheat ordinary cords and strips and start an electrical fire.
  • Turn it off when you leave the room or go to sleep. No space heater is meant to run unattended overnight.
  • Put it on a hard, flat, level surface — not on carpet, a rug, a table, or anything tippy.
  • Use a working smoke alarm, and never use a heater in a wet area like a bathroom unless it's specifically rated for it.

FAQ

Which space heater is safest?
The safest heater is any certified unit (UL, ETL, or CSA) with a tip-over switch, overheat protection, and a thermostat — used correctly. For homes with kids or pets, a cool-touch ceramic or an oil-filled radiator lowers burn risk versus an exposed glowing element. But safe use matters as much as the model: 3 feet of clearance, straight into a wall outlet, and off when you leave or sleep.

How many watts do I need to heat a room?
Roughly 10 watts per square foot. A 1,500W heater suits about a 150 sq ft room. Cold climates, high ceilings, or drafty rooms need more — and since 1,500W is the limit on a standard US circuit, plan to heat one zone rather than a whole large space with a single unit.

Ceramic vs oil-filled — which is better?
Neither is universally better; they're built for different jobs. Ceramic heats fast and is portable, ideal for a quick warm-up or spot heat. Oil-filled is slow to start but gives steady, quiet, longer-lasting warmth, ideal for a room you sit in for hours.

Is it safe to leave a space heater on overnight?
No. NFPA and the U.S. Fire Administration advise turning a space heater off when you go to bed or leave the room. No portable heater is designed to run unattended overnight, regardless of its safety features.

Why can't I plug a space heater into an extension cord or power strip?
A 1,500W heater draws about 12.5 amps, which can overheat a typical extension cord or power strip and cause an electrical fire. Always plug a heater directly into a grounded wall outlet.

Final Takeaway

Choosing a space heater comes down to three decisions: type, wattage, and safety features. Pick the type that matches how you'll use it (ceramic for fast spot heat, oil-filled for steady whole-room warmth, infrared for drafty rooms), size it at about 10 watts per square foot (1,500W for ~150 sq ft), and refuse to buy anything without a certification mark, tip-over switch, and overheat shutoff. Then use it right: 3 feet of clearance, straight into the wall, and off before you sleep. Get those right and a $30–$150 heater will keep one cold room comfortable all winter — safely.
