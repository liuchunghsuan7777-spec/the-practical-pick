Why Are My Plant Leaves Turning Yellow? Causes & Fixes

Quick answer: Most yellow leaves come down to watering — and more often than not, it's overwatering, not under. The fastest way to figure out which problem you have is to look at which leaves are yellow (top or bottom, old or new) and how they feel (soft and mushy, or crispy and dry). From there, the fix usually follows.

Before you panic or toss the plant, take a breath. A few yellow leaves rarely mean a plant is dying. Below you'll find a 30-second diagnosis table, a cause-by-cause walkthrough with fixes, the single most-asked question — overwatered or underwatered? — and clear answers to "should I cut them off?" and "will they turn green again?" Wherever a fix depends on your specific plant, we lean on university extension guidance and tell you to check the soil, not the calendar.

A quick note on certainty: yellowing has a lot of overlapping causes, so treat the table as a starting point, not a guarantee. The texture and location clues below help you narrow it down with more confidence.

Cause #1: Overwatering and root rot (the most common culprit)

If you only read one section, read this one. Overwatering is the leading reason houseplant leaves turn yellow, and it's frequently mistaken for thirst — so people water more, which makes it worse.

When soil stays soggy, the roots can't get oxygen and begin to rot. According to Iowa State University Extension, overwatering symptoms include yellowing and browning leaves, leaf drop, wilting, and fungus gnats, and the wilting often starts at the bottom leaves and moves upward. Iowa State specifically notes that root-rot wilting is "commonly mistaken for a lack of water," so the smart move is to check the roots and soil moisture before adding any more water.

What it looks like: lower leaves yellow first, the leaves feel soft, swollen, or almost translucent, the soil is constantly wet, and the pot may smell sour. Healthy roots are firm and white; rotting roots are brown or reddish and mushy (Iowa State University Extension).

How to fix and prevent it:

  • Stop watering and let the soil dry out before you do anything else.
  • Slide the plant out and check the roots. Trim away any brown, mushy roots with clean scissors.
  • Repot into fresh, well-draining mix if the old soil is compacted and waterlogged.
  • Use a pot with a drainage hole, and never let the plant sit in a saucer of standing water.
  • Going forward, water thoroughly so the whole root ball gets wet, then let it dry down before the next watering.
Best for: stopping root rot
A Pot With Drainage (and the Right Soil)
Overwatering damage almost always traces back to two things: a pot that can't drain and soil that holds too much water. A container with at least one open drainage hole lets excess water escape instead of drowning the roots, and a loose, well-draining houseplant mix keeps air around them. Pros: directly targets the #1 cause of yellow leaves; reusable for years. Cons: repotting is messy and stresses the plant short-term. Match the pot size to the plant (a too-big pot stays wet too long) and never let it sit in a saucer of standing water. Prices vary by material and size, so confirm at the point of sale.

Cause #2: Underwatering

Less common than overwatering, but it happens — especially with fast-draining pots, small pots, or a plant you forgot for a couple of weeks.

What it looks like: an underwatered plant tends to sacrifice its oldest, lowest leaves first, which yellow and then go crispy, papery, and brittle, often with browning that marches in from the leaf tip and edges. The soil pulls away from the sides of the pot, and the plant usually perks up noticeably within hours of a good drink.

How to fix it: give it a deep soak — water until it runs out the drainage hole, or bottom-water by setting the pot in a tray of water until the surface feels moist. Then return to a normal dry-down cycle: water again only when the top inch or two of soil has dried out.

Cause #3: Too little light

Leaves need light to make food. In a spot that's too dim, photosynthesis slows, and the plant can't keep all its foliage green.

What it looks like: the plant looks pale overall rather than in a sharp pattern, growth gets leggy (long stems, sparse leaves), and it visibly leans toward the window. Almanac and Iowa State Extension both list low light — and sudden temperature swings near drafty windows, vents, or doors — as common drivers of yellowing and drooping.

How to fix it: move the plant to a brighter location, ideally near a window with bright indirect light, and rotate it every week or two so it grows evenly. Make the change gradually — a sudden jump from a dark corner to harsh direct sun can scorch leaves.

Cause #4: Nutrient deficiency (location tells you which one)

Here's a clue most people miss: where the yellowing shows up points to which nutrient is short. The rule of thumb from horticulture guides is that mobile nutrients (like nitrogen) get pulled from old leaves to feed new growth, so deficiency shows on old leaves first; immobile nutrients (like iron) can't move, so deficiency shows on new growth first.

Deficiency Where it shows What it looks like General fix
Nitrogen (mobile) Older / bottom leaves first Whole leaf fades to pale yellow — veins included Feed a balanced houseplant fertilizer; refresh soil
Iron (immobile) New / top growth first Interveinal chlorosis — yellow between veins, veins stay green Iron supplement; check that soil pH isn't too high

If the yellow leaf has green veins running through it and it's on new growth, iron-related chlorosis is the classic suspect (per nutrient-deficiency horticulture guides). If whole older leaves, veins and all, fade evenly to pale yellow, nitrogen is the more likely story.

A word of caution: don't reach for fertilizer as a reflex. Overwatered, root-damaged plants often look nutrient-starved because damaged roots can't take up nutrients — and dumping in fertilizer then makes things worse. Rule out watering problems first. When you do feed, keep it generic — "a balanced houseplant fertilizer" or "an iron supplement" — and follow the product label rather than guessing at a dose.

Best for: confirmed nutrient gaps
Balanced Fertilizer vs. Iron Supplement
Once you've ruled out watering and root problems, the right feed depends on the symptom. A balanced houseplant fertilizer covers general pale, fading older leaves (a nitrogen-type pattern); an iron supplement targets new leaves that are yellow with green veins (interveinal chlorosis), often alongside checking soil pH. Pros: corrects a genuine deficiency the plant can't fix on its own. Cons: feeding a plant that's actually overwatered makes things worse, and over-fertilizing can burn roots. Always follow the product label for dose and frequency rather than guessing. Pricing varies by brand and size; confirm current cost at the retailer.

Cause #5: Pests

Tiny insects feeding on your plant can leave yellowing in their wake — and they're easy to overlook because many hide on the undersides of leaves.

What it looks like: yellowing in spots or patches rather than a smooth overall fade, sometimes with fine webbing (a spider mite sign), sticky residue, or visible bugs. Almanac and the University of Minnesota Extension both flag patchy, spotted yellowing as a pest signal.

How to fix it: inspect leaf undersides and stem joints closely — a magnifying glass helps. Isolate the plant from your others so it doesn't spread, wipe leaves down, and treat with an appropriate control such as insecticidal soap, always following the label directions.

Cause #6: Natural aging (sometimes it's nothing)

Not every yellow leaf is a problem. As a plant grows, it redirects energy to new foliage and lets its oldest, lowest leaves yellow and drop. Family Handyman notes this is completely normal.

How to tell it apart: it's just the occasional oldest leaf at the very bottom — not a wave of them, not new growth, and the rest of the plant looks healthy. There's nothing to fix; simply remove the leaf once it's fully yellow if you'd like it tidy.

Overwatered or underwatered? The 4-test cheat sheet

This is the single most-asked sub-question, because the two problems can both cause yellow leaves yet need opposite fixes. Run these quick checks before you decide.

  1. The texture test (fastest). Overwatered leaves feel soft, swollen, sometimes jelly-like or translucent and yellow from the inside out. Underwatered leaves feel crisp, dry, and papery with browning creeping in from the edges (Rockledge Gardens, Urban Roots).
  2. The finger test. Push a finger into the soil to your first knuckle. Moist an inch or two down means don't water. Dry down there means it's probably thirsty. Constantly wet soil — especially with a sour smell — points to overwatering (Rockledge Gardens).
  3. The root check. Firm and white = healthy. Brown, reddish, and mushy = root rot from too much water (Iowa State University Extension).
  4. The recovery test. A plant that perks up fast after watering was simply thirsty. One that stays limp while the soil is still wet was overwatered.

When in doubt, err on the side of less water. It's far easier to rescue a thirsty plant than one whose roots have rotted.

Should I cut off yellow leaves? Will they turn green again?

Will they turn green again? No. Once a leaf has gone fully yellow, it has lost its chlorophyll (a condition called chlorosis), and that damage is permanent — it won't re-green (Gardening Mentor, Houseplant101). A leaf that's only partly yellow still has working green tissue, so you can leave it.

Should I cut them off? Only the fully yellow ones (no green left). Removing them redirects the plant's energy to healthy new growth and improves air circulation. Cut at the base, where the leaf meets the stem, with clean scissors to avoid spreading disease — and remember, pruning is cosmetic. It doesn't fix the underlying cause, so always address the why (water, light, nutrients, pests) too.

Plant-specific quick notes

Different houseplants have different "usual suspects." A few common ones:

  • Snake plant: almost always overwatering — they're drought-tough and rot easily in soggy soil. Let them dry out well between waterings.
  • Pothos: typically overwatering or too little light; check the soil moisture and the spot first.
  • Peace lily: dramatic droopers when thirsty, but persistent yellowing often means overwatering or harsh light. Move it out of direct sun and ease up on the water.

FAQ

Why are my plant's leaves turning yellow?
Most often it's a watering issue — usually overwatering. Check which leaves are affected and how they feel, then match them to the diagnosis table above; light, nutrients, pests, and natural aging are the other common causes.

Is yellowing a sign of overwatering or underwatering?
It can be either. Overwatered leaves are soft and mushy with constantly wet soil; underwatered leaves are crispy and dry with bone-dry soil that pulls away from the pot. The texture and finger tests are the quickest way to tell.

Will yellow leaves turn green again?
A fully yellow leaf will not — the chlorophyll loss is permanent. A partly green leaf can stay on the plant and keep contributing.

Should I cut off yellow leaves?
Yes, but only the fully yellow ones, cut at the base. It tidies the plant and redirects energy to new growth, but it doesn't fix the root cause.

Why are only the bottom leaves turning yellow?
Bottom-leaf yellowing commonly points to overwatering/root rot, natural aging, or nitrogen deficiency. Use texture and soil-moisture clues to tell them apart.

Why are the new leaves yellow but the veins still green?
That pattern (interveinal chlorosis on new growth) is the classic sign of an iron-related deficiency, often tied to soil pH being too high for the plant to take up iron.

Before you panic: a 5-step checklist

  1. Check the soil moisture — finger to the first knuckle. Soggy or bone-dry?
  2. Check the pot — is there a working drainage hole, or is water pooling?
  3. Check the light — too dim, or suddenly too harsh?
  4. Check which leaves — old/bottom vs new/top, and how they feel.
  5. Check for pests — flip the leaves over and look at the undersides.

Work through those five and you'll usually find your answer. Remember the golden rule for houseplants: check the soil, not the calendar — watering needs change with the season, the pot, the light, and the plant.
