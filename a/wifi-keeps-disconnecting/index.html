Why Does My Wi-Fi Keep Disconnecting? Causes & Fixes

Quick answer: Most Wi-Fi disconnects are fixed by power-cycling your modem and router — unplug them, wait about 60 seconds, and plug them back in. If only one device keeps dropping while others stay online, the problem is that device, not your Wi-Fi. Below you'll find the fast fixes first, then a cause-by-cause breakdown for your router, Windows PC, phone, and Mac.

There are few things more maddening than your Wi-Fi cutting out in the middle of a video call, a game, or a download. The good news: most of the time it's something you can fix yourself in a few minutes — no technician required. This guide gives you the "try this first" steps, a 30-second test to find the real culprit, and device-specific fixes for Windows, Mac, iPhone, and Android.

Heads-up: router menus, firmware screens, and phone settings change between versions. The steps here are current as of September 2026 and kept brand-generic on purpose — always check the exact labels in your own router's app or your device's current settings.

First, try these 5 quick fixes

Before you go deep, run through this short list. One of these clears the problem the large majority of the time.

  1. Power-cycle your modem, then your router. Unplug the power cable, wait about 60 seconds, and plug it back in. If you have a separate modem and router, restart the modem first — it sits at the edge of your provider's network — then the router. This clears temporary errors and miscommunication between the two. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, HowStuffWorks.)
  2. Move closer to the router. If the drops happen far away or across floors, weak signal is the likely cause. Get within line of sight and see if it holds.
  3. Forget the network and rejoin it. On the device that's dropping, remove the saved Wi-Fi network and reconnect with the password. This clears a corrupted saved profile.
  4. Reconnect to the 5 GHz network if your router offers separate 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz names. 5 GHz is usually less crowded for nearby devices (more on this below).
  5. Check for an ISP outage. Open your provider's app or status page, or check on mobile data. Sometimes it's genuinely not your equipment. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, NetSpot.)

If the problem survives all five, the next section tells you exactly where to look.

Is it one device or your whole network? (the 30-second test)

This is the single smartest thing you can do, and it takes half a minute. Watch what happens across your devices the next time Wi-Fi drops:

  • Every device drops at the same time → the problem is your router, modem, or ISP. Focus on the router-side and provider fixes.
  • Only one device drops while the others stay connected → the problem is that device — its Wi-Fi driver, a power-saving setting, or its saved network profile. (Source: Microsoft Q&A, NetSpot.)

That one observation routes you straight to the right section and can save you hours of poking at the wrong thing. Note which bucket you're in, then jump to the matching fix below.

Why your Wi-Fi keeps disconnecting — the 7 real causes

If the quick fixes didn't hold, one of these is usually behind it.

  1. Weak signal / distance. Distance and physical obstructions create coverage gaps, especially in large homes or across multiple floors. Weak signal is one of the most common causes of an unstable connection. (Source: NetSpot.)
  2. Interference and a crowded band. The 2.4 GHz band is far busier — more neighbors and devices use it, and microwaves and Bluetooth gear add interference. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, NetSpot.)
  3. Channel congestion. Neighboring routers on overlapping Wi-Fi channels fight for airspace. Switching to a less congested channel (a Wi-Fi analyzer app shows which are busy) can stabilize things. (Source: NetSpot.)
  4. Too many devices / overload. Even idle devices sip bandwidth, and heavy activity — streaming, gaming, backups, big updates — can overwhelm an older router. As a rough planning guideline, one source suggests budgeting around 100 Mbps per active person and comparing that to your plan speed. Treat that as a rule of thumb, not a hard rule. (Source: HowStuffWorks, NetSpot, HighSpeedInternet.)
  5. Outdated firmware. Router firmware updates patch bugs and security holes; running old firmware is a common cause of instability. (Source: NetSpot, iFeelTech.)
  6. Overheating or aging hardware. Heat and old components degrade performance and can trigger surprise reboots. Make sure the router has ventilation and isn't shut in a cabinet. (Source: NetSpot.)
  7. Your ISP. A provider outage or maintenance at your address can look exactly like a home problem. Contact your ISP — they keep detailed records of disruptions. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, NetSpot.)

Loose cables deserve a mention too: make sure coaxial connectors are screwed on snug and Ethernet plugs click fully into the port, and check for kinks or damage. (Source: HighSpeedInternet.)

Fix Wi-Fi that keeps disconnecting on Windows (10 & 11)

If only your Windows PC drops while everything else stays online, two settings fix it most of the time.

1. Update the Wi-Fi adapter driver. Outdated or corrupted Wi-Fi drivers are a top cause of disconnects on Windows 10 and 11. Update the driver through Device Manager or your laptop maker's support page. (Source: NetSpot, Microsoft Q&A.)

2. Stop Windows from powering down the Wi-Fi adapter. A power-saving setting can switch your adapter off and cause repeated drops:

  • Open Device Manager (right-click Start, or search for it).
  • Expand Network adapters and right-click your Wi-Fi adapter.
  • Choose Properties > Power Management tab.
  • Uncheck "Allow the computer to turn off this device to save power," then OK. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, Microsoft Q&A.)

Menu labels shift slightly between Windows builds, so if the wording differs a little, look for the equivalent option.

Fix Wi-Fi drops on iPhone, Android & Mac

When the dropping device is a phone, tablet, or Mac, these generic steps work across iOS, Android, and macOS — exact menu names vary by version:

  • Toggle Airplane mode off and on, or turn Wi-Fi off and back on, to force a clean reconnect.
  • Restart the device. A simple reboot clears a stuck radio or background glitch.
  • Forget the network and rejoin it. This wipes a bad saved profile — a frequent cause of one-device drops. (Source: NetSpot.)
  • Disable Wi-Fi auto-join / auto-switching. Features that let a device hop between networks or bands can drop the connection mid-use; connect to your preferred network manually instead. (Source: HowStuffWorks.)
  • Update the OS. Keep iOS, Android, or macOS current — connectivity bugs are often fixed in updates. (Source: NetSpot.)

2.4 GHz vs 5 GHz — which should you connect to?

Most modern routers broadcast two bands, and picking the right one stops a surprising number of drops.

  • 5 GHz: faster and far less crowded, but its range is shorter and it's weaker through walls. Best for devices near the router.
  • 2.4 GHz: slower and more congested (neighbors, microwaves, Bluetooth), but it reaches farther and penetrates walls better. Best for devices far from the router.

So as a general rule: if a nearby device keeps dropping, connect it to 5 GHz; if a far-away device drops, 2.4 GHz reaches it but expect more interference. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, NetSpot.) If you're in a crowded apartment building and both bands struggle, switching to a less congested channel — found with a free Wi-Fi analyzer app — often helps. Exact behavior varies by router, so treat this as guidance, not a guarantee.


A quick way to remember it: near the router, pick 5 GHz; far away, 2.4 GHz reaches farther.

Router placement that stops drop-outs

Where you put the router matters more than most people expect. A few placement rules fix range-related drops on their own:

  • Keep it central, high, and out in the open — not tucked in a corner, a cabinet, or behind the TV.
  • Avoid metal furniture, bathrooms, and fish tanks. Metal and water absorb or block the signal. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, HowStuffWorks.)
  • Don't enclose it. Give it airflow so it doesn't overheat.
  • For real dead zones, add coverage. If parts of a large home simply don't reach, a mesh Wi-Fi system or a Wi-Fi extender can fix drops caused purely by range. (Source: NetSpot.)

Do you need a new router? (signs it's time)

If your router is several years old and nothing above sticks, the hardware itself may be the limit. Here's how to tell — and a balanced look at the upgrade question rather than a sales pitch.

General guidelines from the makers and reviewers:

  • Most routers last about 3–5 years. Consider replacing sooner if it runs outdated Wi-Fi standards or has stopped receiving software updates. (Source: Netgear, How-To Geek.)
  • Support windows matter. Vendors typically support a model for about 3–5 years after release; budget models may stop getting updates after roughly 2 years, while top brands offer 5–7. An unsupported router is both a security and a stability risk. (Source: How-To Geek, Reolink.)
  • If your router predates Wi-Fi 6, upgrading usually gives a noticeable real-world improvement for most households, and a Wi-Fi 6 router bought today should stay relevant for several years. (Source: How-To Geek.)

Signs it's time: frequent unexplained drops on a 4–5+ year-old router, no firmware updates available, it can't keep up with your plan speed or device count, or it has no 5 GHz support at all. (Source: HighSpeedInternet, Netgear.)

FAQ

Why does my Wi-Fi disconnect every few minutes?
A repeating, every-few-minutes drop usually points to either a power-saving setting cutting off a device's Wi-Fi adapter, interference on a crowded channel, or aging router hardware. Start by checking whether it's one device or all of them — that tells you whether to fix the device (driver, power management) or the router (channel, firmware, placement).

Why does my Wi-Fi drop on my phone but not my laptop?
Because the problem is on the phone, not the network. Forget and rejoin the Wi-Fi network, toggle Airplane mode, turn off Wi-Fi auto-join, and update the phone's OS. A bad saved profile or an auto-switching feature is the usual cause.

Does restarting the router really help?
Yes, surprisingly often. Power-cycling (unplug ~60 seconds, plug back in) clears temporary errors and refreshes the link between your modem and router. Restart the modem first, then the router, if they're separate boxes.

Is 2.4 GHz or 5 GHz better?
Neither is "better" everywhere. 5 GHz is faster and less congested but shorter-range — best for devices near the router. 2.4 GHz reaches farther and through walls but is more crowded — best for distant devices. Use both: connect each device to whichever fits its location.

How often should I replace my router?
As a general guideline, about every 3–5 years, or sooner if it no longer receives firmware updates, can't keep up with your plan or device count, or predates Wi-Fi 6. An unsupported router is a security and stability risk.

Can a Wi-Fi extender stop disconnects?
It can, if your drops are caused by weak signal or dead zones — an extender or a mesh system fills coverage gaps. It won't help if the cause is a dying router, old firmware, ISP outages, or a single misbehaving device, so diagnose the cause first.

The bottom line

  • Whole network dropping? Power-cycle the modem then the router, and rule out an ISP outage.
  • One device dropping? Fix that device — update its Wi-Fi driver or OS, turn off power-saving, and forget-and-rejoin the network.
  • Drops far from the router? It's range — move the router central and high, and add a mesh system or extender for true dead zones.

Nine times out of ten, a 60-second power-cycle plus moving the router into the open does it. Work down the list above, use the 30-second one-device-or-all test to aim your effort, and you'll have a stable connection again without a service call.
