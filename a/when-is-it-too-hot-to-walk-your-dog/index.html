When Is It Too Hot to Walk Your Dog? Vet Safety Guide

If you've typed "is it too hot to walk my dog?" into a search bar on a sweltering afternoon, here's the short version.

It's generally too hot to walk your dog when the pavement fails the 7-second hand test — usually around midday in summer. Walk in the early morning or later evening instead, and stick to grass or shade. Dogs handle heat far worse than people do, and a single midday walk can lead to burned paw pads or heatstroke.

This guide covers safe walk times, how to cool your dog down, the heatstroke warning signs to watch for, and exactly what to do if your dog overheats — all grounded in guidance from veterinary sources.

A quick note before we start: this is general pet-care information, not a veterinary diagnosis. Every dog is different, so when something seems off, call your own vet.

Why dogs overheat faster than people

People sweat across the whole body to shed heat. Dogs can't. They have very few sweat glands, so they cool off mainly by panting — breathing fast with the tongue out to evaporate moisture from the mouth and airway.

Panting is far less efficient than sweating, and it nearly stops working in hot, humid weather. That's why dogs tip into trouble much faster than we do. Heatstroke in a dog is a true medical emergency that can cause organ damage and death in under an hour (American Kennel Club; Cornell Riney Canine Health Center).

Rather than chasing an exact "danger temperature," it's more useful to watch your dog: panting that's heavier or faster than usual, plus sluggishness, is your real-world signal that the heat is too much.

Which dogs are most at risk

Some dogs are far more vulnerable than others:

  • Flat-faced (brachycephalic) breeds — Bulldogs, French Bulldogs, and Pugs. Their short airways make it much harder to cool down by panting. A large UK study of more than 900,000 dogs (the Royal Veterinary College's VetCompass project) compared breeds to the Labrador Retriever and found the English Bulldog is about 14 times more likely to develop heatstroke than a Labrador, and the French Bulldog about 6 times more likely. Across all dogs that developed heatstroke, about 1 in 7 died (RVC VetCompass / Nottingham Trent University, 2020).
  • Overweight dogs, dogs over 2 years old, and dogs that are large for their breed also carry extra risk, according to the same study.
  • Senior dogs and dogs with heart or breathing conditions are generally more vulnerable too, so plan their outings conservatively.

When is it too hot to walk your dog?

The best time: early morning or later evening

This is the single most important rule. In summer, walk your dog early in the morning (before about 10 a.m.) or later in the evening (after about 7 p.m.), when both the air and the ground are coolest. Avoid the midday-to-late-afternoon peak, when temperatures are highest (AKC).

Even just after sunset, asphalt can stay hot for hours because it soaks up heat all day. Don't rely on the clock alone — check the ground with the hand test below.

How hot is too hot for dogs' paws?

Pavement runs much hotter than the air. When the air is around 86°F (30°C), asphalt can climb to roughly 135°F (57°C), and on a 90°F (32°C) day it can reach about 150°F (65°C) (AKC; FOUR PAWS). On surfaces that hot, a dog's paw pads can burn in about 60 seconds (AKC; ElleVet Sciences).

Signs of a paw-pad burn include limping, licking or chewing the paws, and pads that look red, darkened, or blistered.

The 7-second test is the simplest safety check:

Press the back of your hand flat against the pavement. If you can't hold it there comfortably for 7 seconds, it's too hot for your dog's paws (Vets Now; The Kennel Club). Some sources use a 5- to 10-second version — when in doubt, treat it as too hot.

A few more pointers:

  • Choose grass, dirt paths, or shaded routes over asphalt, which always runs cooler.
  • Pavement can be risky even on moderate days — bright sun on a 77°F (25°C) day can still overheat a surface, so use the test, not just the thermometer (AKC).
  • Keep summer walks short and easy, bring water, and rest often in the shade.

Keeping your dog cool in summer

Walk timing matters, but so does heat management for the rest of the day. The foundation is simple: a cool space and plenty of water.

  • Shade and a cool indoor space. Use AC or fans and keep an out-of-the-sun spot available. Rather than fixate on an exact thermostat number (sources differ and dogs vary), aim for a level where your dog can rest comfortably without panting (AKC).
  • Constant access to fresh water. This is your main defense against dehydration. Keep several water bowls around the house, and bring a portable bottle on walks and outings (AKC; PetMD).
  • Cooling gear is a supportive aid, not a substitute for a cool environment, water, and rest. Here's how the common options compare.

Dog heatstroke warning signs and what to do

When you think "my dog might be overheating," the first step is recognizing the warning signs.

Signs of heatstroke in dogs

Early signs:

  • Heavy, rapid panting (rougher and faster than usual)
  • Excessive drooling, often thick or sticky
  • Bright red gums and tongue
  • Skin that feels hot to the touch, elevated heart rate
  • Weakness, wobbling, or seeming dazed

As it worsens: vomiting, diarrhea (sometimes bloody), disorientation, collapse, seizures, or loss of consciousness (AKC; PetMD; Cornell).


Dog heatstroke warning signs to watch for — cool with room-temperature water (never ice) and get to a vet immediately.

First aid while you head to the vet

These are temporary steps to take on the way to the vet — not a replacement for treatment. Don't delay by trying to handle it at home. Call an emergency vet and go right away.

  1. Move your dog to shade or a cool indoor space immediately.
  2. Wet your dog with cool, room-temperature water — not ice-cold — over the paws, belly, armpits, and neck. Drape on wet towels and aim a fan at them so the temperature comes down gradually.
  3. Call your vet or an emergency clinic now and bring your dog in; call ahead so they're ready.

Do not use ice or ice-cold water. Sudden, extreme cooling can constrict blood vessels and make things worse (Cornell; PetMD). Cool, room-temperature water and a gradual drop is the goal — and the specific treatment should follow your vet's direction. Some veterinary sources report that starting to cool a dog before arrival meaningfully improves survival, which is why these early steps matter so much. Bad first-aid information can harm the animal, so when in doubt, let the vet guide you.

Never leave your dog in a hot car

"I'll only be a minute" is exactly how dogs die in parked cars. Never leave a dog in a parked car, even briefly. The interior turns deadly fast.

  • On a 70°F (21°C) day, a car's interior can reach about 99°F (37°C) in 20 minutes; on a 90°F (32°C) day it can hit around 109°F (43°C) in just 10 minutes. Interiors can rise roughly 20°F in 10 minutes and 40°F in an hour (Vets Now; FOUR PAWS).
  • Cracking the windows does not meaningfully help. A car with the windows cracked heats up at almost the same rate (AKC; AVMA via Vets Now).
  • Dogs can suffer fatal heatstroke in as little as 15 minutes in a hot car (Vets Now; PETA).

A few related don'ts for hot days:

  • Flat-faced, senior, overweight, or heart-condition dogs overheat faster, so be even more conservative about walks and outings.
  • Skip strenuous exercise — long walks and hard fetch sessions — when it's hot.

Frequently asked questions

How hot is too hot to walk a dog?
There's no single cutoff temperature, because humidity, ground heat, breed, and age all matter. The practical answer is to go by walk timing (early morning or later evening) and the 7-second pavement test rather than a thermometer alone. For flat-faced and senior dogs, be even more cautious.

How can I tell if the pavement is too hot?
Press the back of your hand flat on the pavement. If you can't hold it there comfortably for 7 seconds, it's too hot for your dog's paws — delay the walk or switch to grass and shade.

Should I put ice on my overheating dog?
No. Ice and ice-cold water can constrict blood vessels and backfire. Use cool, room-temperature water on the paws, belly, and armpits to lower the temperature gradually, and call your vet immediately. Follow your vet's instructions for treatment.

Can flat-faced dogs walk in summer?
It's not forbidden, but it needs extra care. Walk during the coolest hours, keep it short and shaded, offer water often, and watch your dog closely. At the first sign of rough panting or sluggishness, stop and move to a cool spot right away.
