How to Choose a Printer (Inkjet, Laser & All-in-One)

Inkjet vs laser, ink costs, and all-in-one features — pick a home printer that won't cost a fortune to run.

Buying a printer sounds simple until you're staring at a wall of options and specs that don't mean much on their own. The right pick depends on what you print, how often, and whether ink or toner costs will sneak up on you over time. This guide walks through the main types and the features that actually matter so you can match a machine to your real needs.

Inkjet vs. laser: the core decision

The first fork in the road is inkjet versus laser, and it comes down to what you print most.

Inkjet printers push liquid ink onto the page and shine when it comes to color, gradients, and photos. If you print school projects, greeting cards, or the occasional picture, an inkjet handles the range of tones well. A dedicated photo printer takes this further, using extra ink colors to render smoother skin tones and richer detail than a general-purpose model.

Laser printers use toner powder fused to the page with heat. They excel at crisp black text and high volume. If most of what you print is documents, forms, and shipping labels, a laser printer will feel faster and steadier, and the pages tend to resist smudging and fading better than inkjet output.

What to look for

Once you've narrowed the type, a handful of features separate a good buy from a frustrating one.

Running cost of ink or toner. The sticker price is only part of the story. Inkjet cartridges hold less and get used up faster, so a cheap printer can cost more over its life. Toner cartridges cost more upfront but print far more pages before you replace them, which usually means a lower cost per page for heavy users. Look at the rated page yield of the cartridges, not just the printer price.

All-in-one scan and copy. An all-in-one printer combines printing with a scanner, so it can also copy and, on many models, fax. If you deal with paperwork, having a flatbed or a document feeder built in saves you from owning a separate device. It's often the single most useful upgrade for a home office.

Wireless. Wi-Fi lets you print from a laptop, phone, or tablet without cables, and many printers support printing directly from mobile apps. If more than one person will use the machine, or you want to print from across the room, wireless is close to essential.

Duplex printing. Automatic duplex prints on both sides of the page without you flipping the paper. It cuts paper use roughly in half for long documents and gives reports and handouts a cleaner, more finished feel. Confirm it's automatic rather than manual.

Page speed. Measured in pages per minute, speed matters most if you print in bursts or large batches. For a few pages here and there, almost any printer is fast enough. If you routinely print long documents, a faster rated speed is worth paying for.

Paper handling. Check the input tray capacity and the paper sizes supported. A bigger tray means fewer refills, and support for envelopes, labels, or heavier stock matters if your work calls for it.

Who should buy what

Match the machine to how you actually live and work.

If you print mostly text documents and want low costs at volume, a laser printer is the practical choice, especially for a home office that runs invoices, contracts, or study materials day after day.

If you have a mixed household with kids' assignments, forms to sign, and occasional color, an all-in-one printer covers the widest range. The built-in scanner and copier earn their keep, and wireless makes it easy for the whole family to share.

If image quality is your priority and you frame prints, sell artwork, or archive family memories, a photo printer is worth the specialized ink and slower pace. For everyday documents on the side, you may still want a second, cheaper workhorse.

Light users who print only now and then should focus on low idle upkeep and reliable wireless rather than raw speed. A simple all-in-one often fits this pattern best.

Watch the hidden costs

Beyond cartridges, think about longevity. Printers that sit unused can clog inkjet nozzles, so if you print rarely, a laser or a pigment-based design tends to be more forgiving. Also consider whether replacement cartridges are easy to find and whether the model supports high-yield versions, which stretch each dollar further over the years you'll own it.

Bottom line

Start with what you print. Choose a laser printer for high-volume text, an all-in-one printer for versatile home use with scanning and copying, and a photo printer when picture quality leads. Then weigh the running cost of ink or toner, plus wireless, duplex, and page speed, so the machine you bring home keeps serving you long after the box is unpacked.
